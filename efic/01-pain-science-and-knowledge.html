Section 1: Pain Science and Knowledge — EFIC Ingest & Study Guide

This module covers the core theoretical, biological, philosophical, and systemic foundations required for Section One: Pain Science and Knowledge of the official EFIC Core Curriculum.

1. Theories & Philosophical Approaches to Pain (Curriculum 1.1)

A. Philosophical Foundations & Epistemic Frameworks (1.1.2)

  • Cartesian Dualism (René Descartes, 1664):
  • Conceptualized pain as a direct, mechanical pull on a string leading from the damaged tissue (e.g., foot in fire) directly to an alarm bell in the brain.
  • Philosophical Flaw: Strict separation of Mind (res cogitans) and Body (res extensa). Leads to the false biomedical dichotomy: "pain is either organic (tissue injury) or psychogenic (in the head)".
  • Curriculum Rebuttal: Dualism fails because pain is non-linear, centrally modulated, and inherently biopsychosocial.
  • Monism vs. Dualism:
  • Monism (Physicalism / Identity Theory): Mind and brain are one biological continuum; subjective experiences of pain arise from physical neural processing without requiring an immaterial soul.
  • Enactive / Embodied Cognition (4E Cognition): Pain is not merely an internal neural representation, but an embodied, embedded, enacted, and extended sense-making process of the whole organism interacting with its physical and social environment.
  • Reductionism vs. Biopsychosocial Holism (George Engel, 1977):
  • Biomedical Reductionism: Assumes all pain can be reduced to nociceptive input, mechanical tissue fault, or biochemical pathology.
  • Biopsychosocial Holism: Biological nociception is filtered through, modulated by, and inseparable from psychological appraisals (meaning, catastrophizing, fear) and sociocultural contexts (workplace, disability policy, relationships, marginalization).
  • Epistemic Injustice & Pain Validation (Fricker / EFIC Curriculum 1.1.2 & 1.2.2):
  • Testimonial Injustice: Occurs when a clinician affords less credibility to a patient's report of pain due to prejudices (e.g., gender bias dismissing female pelvic pain as "emotional", racial bias under-prescribing analgesia, or dismissing nociplastic pain as "malingering" because scans are clear).
  • Hermeneutical Injustice: Occurs when a patient lacks the shared conceptual resources or language to make their suffering intelligible to healthcare providers (e.g., struggling to describe nociplastic symptoms within a strictly structural-pathology biomedical model).
  • Clinical Rule: Pain is an inherently subjective first-person experience; genuine validation of the patient's lived experience is a clinical and ethical prerequisite.

B. Evolution of Theoretical Models in Pain Science (1.1.1)

  1. Specificity Theory (von Frey, 1894): Hypothesized dedicated pain receptors and distinct pathways directly to a pain center in the brain. Overturned because it cannot explain central modulation, phantom pain, or allodynia.
  2. Pattern Theory (Goldscheider, 1894): Proposed that pain results from spatial and temporal summation of non-specific somatic receptors. Incomplete, but anticipated summation mechanisms.
  3. Gate Control Theory (Melzack & Wall, 1965):
  4. Located in the substantia gelatinosa (Lamina II of the spinal dorsal horn).
  5. Large-diameter non-nociceptive $A\beta$ fibers activate inhibitory interneurons, "closing the gate" to spinal transmission ($T$) cells.
  6. Small $A\delta$ and $C$ nociceptive fibers inhibit the inhibitory interneurons, "opening the gate".
  7. Incorporates descending top-down modulation from cortical and brainstem structures (attention, emotion, anticipation).
  8. Neuromatrix Theory of Pain (Ronald Melzack, 1999):
  9. Pain is produced by a widely distributed brain neural network (the body-self neuromatrix) comprising:
    • Sensory-discriminative dimension ($S1, S2$, lateral thalamus).
    • Affective-motivational dimension (limbic system, anterior insula, ACC, amygdala).
    • Cognitive-evaluative dimension (prefrontal cortices, posterior parietal cortex).
  10. Generates a characteristic neurochemical/neural output called a neurosignature. The neuromatrix can produce pain in the total absence of peripheral inputs (e.g., phantom limb pain).
  11. Mature Organism Model (Louis Gifford, 1998):
  12. Organism operates in a continuous survival loop:
    • Input: Tissues of the body and environmental context.
    • Scrutiny / Central Processing: Sampling mechanisms, brain scrutiny, memory, and threat appraisal ("Is it safe or dangerous?").
    • Output: Behavioral, physiological, motor, autonomic, neuroendocrine, and immune responses designed for protection and adaptation.
  13. Predictive Processing & The Bayesian Brain Model (Friston, Clark, Tabor, Moseley):
  14. The brain is not a passive stimulus-receiver, but an active prediction engine.
  15. The brain continuously generates top-down generative models (prior expectations, priors) regarding bodily threat and sensory states.
  16. Incoming nociceptive inputs serve as sensory evidence that generate prediction errors.
  17. Pain emerges when the brain concludes that the body is under threat based on the integration of prior expectations and sensory prediction errors, weighted by precision (confidence). Chronic nociplastic pain reflects overly precise threat priors that resist bottom-up sensory disconfirmation.

2. Neurobiology of Nociception & Pain Mechanisms (1.1.3 & 1.1.4)

A. Primary Afferents & Peripheral Sensors (1.1.3)

  • Primary Afferent Classes:
  • $A\beta$ fibers: Large diameter ($6\text{–}12\ \mu\text{m}$), heavily myelinated, rapid conduction velocity ($30\text{–}70\text{ m/s}$). Low-threshold mechanoreceptors (light touch, vibration, proprioception). Normally non-nociceptive; during central sensitization, can activate $T$-cells via phenotypic switching / disinhibition to trigger dynamic mechanical allodynia.
  • $A\delta$ fibers: Medium diameter ($1\text{–}5\ \mu\text{m}$), lightly myelinated, intermediate conduction velocity ($5\text{–}30\text{ m/s}$). High-threshold mechanoreceptors and mechano-thermal nociceptors. Mediate "first pain" (sharp, well-localized, rapid pricking pain; triggers withdrawal reflex).
  • $C$ fibers: Small diameter ($0.2\text{–}1.5\ \mu\text{m}$), unmyelinated, slow conduction velocity ($0.5\text{–}2\text{ m/s}$). Mediate "second pain" (dull, burning, diffuse, prolonged aching pain).
  • Subclasses of $C$-Fibers (Crucial for EFIC MCQ):
  • Peptidergic $C$-fibers:
    • Express TrkA (Nerve Growth Factor / NGF high-affinity receptor).
    • Contain neuropeptides: Substance P and Calcitonin Gene-Related Peptide (CGRP).
    • Mediate neurogenic inflammation via antidromic axon reflex: CGRP causes profound arteriolar vasodilation (flare), while Substance P causes vascular permeability and plasma extravasation (wheal/edema).
  • Non-peptidergic $C$-fibers:
    • Bind isolectin $B4$ ($IB4$), express purinergic receptors ($P2X_3$), and Ret (GDNF receptor). Do not produce classic neurogenic flare.
  • $C$-Tactile ($CT$) fibers:
    • Unmyelinated, slowly conducting low-threshold mechanoreceptors present exclusively in hairy skin.
    • Respond preferentially to slow, gentle stroking ($1\text{–}10\text{ cm/s}$) at human skin temperature; convey pleasant, social, affective touch to the insular cortex (NOT primary somatosensory cortex).
  • Peripheral Nociceptor Transduction Receptors:
  • TRPV1 (Transient Receptor Potential Vanilloid 1): Heat sensor ($>43^\circ\text{C}$), capsaicin, and acidic $\text{pH}$ ($<5.9$). Sensitized by inflammatory mediators ($\text{PGE}_2$, bradykinin, NGF).
  • TRPM8: Cool/cold sensor ($<25^\circ\text{C}$) and menthol.
  • TRPA1: Noxious cold ($<17^\circ\text{C}$), mustard oil, formalin, environmental irritants.
  • ASICs (Acid-Sensing Ion Channels): Detect tissue ischemia, acidosis, and muscular fatigue.
  • Voltage-Gated Sodium Channels: $\text{Nav}1.7, \text{Nav}1.8, \text{Nav}1.9$ (loss of $\text{Nav}1.7$ function causes congenital insensitivity to pain; gain-of-function causes erythromelalgia).

B. Spinal Dorsal Horn Laminae & Ascending Tracts

  • Rexed Laminae Architecture:
  • Lamina I (Marginal Zone): Receives direct nociceptive input from $A\delta$ and peptidergic $C$ fibers. Contains nociceptive-specific (NS) projection neurons.
  • Lamina II (Substantia Gelatinosa): Dense network of excitatory and inhibitory (GABAergic / glycinergic) interneurons. Essential site of Gate Control.
  • Lamina III & IV (Nucleus Proprius): Receive primarily non-noxious $A\beta$ inputs (touch, pressure).
  • Lamina V: Contains Wide Dynamic Range (WDR) neurons. WDR neurons receive convergent monosynaptic inputs from $A\beta$, $A\delta$, and polysynaptic inputs from $C$ fibers, as well as visceral afferents (anatomical basis for viscerosomatic referred pain). WDR neurons undergo wind-up.
  • Ascending Tracts:
  • Spinothalamic Tract (STT):
    • Lateral STT: Projects to ventral posterolateral (VPL) and ventral posteromedial (VPM) nuclei of the thalamus $\rightarrow$ $S1$ and $S2$. Encodes sensory-discriminative dimensions (location, intensity, duration, quality).
    • Medial STT: Projects to medial thalamic nuclei (central lateral, parafascicular) $\rightarrow$ Anterior Cingulate Cortex (ACC) and insula. Encodes affective-motivational dimensions.
  • Spinoreticular Tract: Projects to brainstem reticular formation (mediates motor arousal, autonomic reflexes, and sleep disruption).
  • Spinomesencephalic Tract: Projects to the Periaqueductal Gray (PAG) to engage endogenous descending feedback loops.
  • Spinoparabrachial Tract: Projects to the parabrachial area $\rightarrow$ amygdala and hypothalamus (mediates fear, aversion, neuroendocrine stress response).

C. Descending Modulation, Endogenous Analgesia & DNIC/CPM

  • PAG-RVM Axis (Periaqueductal Gray $\rightarrow$ Rostroventromedial Medulla $\rightarrow$ Dorsal Horn):
  • High density of endogenous opioid peptides (endorphins, enkephalins, dynorphins) and $\mu$-opioid receptors.
  • RVM Neurons:
    • OFF-cells: Display a burst of firing immediately preceding analgesia; inhibit spinal nociceptive transmission. Activated by opioids and cannabinoids.
    • ON-cells: Display a burst of firing immediately preceding nociceptive reflexes; facilitate spinal nociceptive transmission. Inactivated by opioids.
    • NEUTRAL-cells: Fire continuously without modulation.
  • Neurotransmitters: Descending inhibition uses Serotonin ($5\text{-HT}$ via $5\text{-HT}_1$ receptors) and Noradrenaline (from Locus Coeruleus via $\alpha_2$-adrenergic receptors).
  • Diffuse Noxious Inhibitory Controls (DNIC / CPM - Conditioned Pain Modulation):
  • Principle: "Pain inhibits pain" — application of a conditioning noxious stimulus at a remote body region (e.g., hand in ice water) inhibits pain perception from a local test stimulus (e.g., pressure algometry on the leg).
  • Anatomical Circuit: Spino-bulbo-spinal loop mediated through the subnucleus reticularis dorsalis (SRD) in the caudal medulla.
  • Clinical Significance: Deficient or absent CPM is a hallmark of central sensitization and predicts post-surgical chronification (Yarnitsky 2012).

D. Peripheral vs. Central Sensitization & Wind-Up (1.1.4)

  • Peripheral Sensitization:
  • Primary hyperalgesia confined to the site of tissue injury.
  • Mediated by the "inflammatory soup" ($\text{PGE}_2$, bradykinin, serotonin, histamine, NGF, $\text{H}^+$, $\text{IL}-1\beta, \text{TNF}-\alpha$).
  • Nociceptor transduction threshold is lowered; spontaneous firing occurs.
  • The "Wind-Up" Phenomenon (Homosynaptic Frequency-Dependent Summation):
  • Location: Spinal dorsal horn WDR neurons.
  • Trigger: Repetitive, low-frequency ($0.5\text{–}2\text{ Hz}$) stimulation of peripheral unmyelinated $C$-fibers.
  • Mechanism: Slow clearance of Substance P and CGRP causes sustained depolarization, opening the $Mg^{2+}$ gate on NMDA receptors.
  • Characteristics: Homosynaptic (confined to the stimulated pathway); Short duration (abates seconds after stimulus stops).
  • Human Psychophysical Correlate: Temporal Summation of Pain (TSP).
  • Central Sensitization (Heterosynaptic Long-Term Potentiation / LTP):
  • Location & Target Neurons: Central nociceptive neurons (never peripheral afferents) — primarily spinal dorsal horn lamina V Wide Dynamic Range (WDR) and lamina I nociceptive-specific (NS) projection neurons, plus lamina II excitatory/inhibitory interneurons; also involves supraspinal amplification (RVM ON-cell facilitation, thalamus, limbic/cortical networks).
  • Mechanism: Massive intracellular calcium influx via unblocked NMDA receptors $\rightarrow$ activation of Protein Kinase C ($PKC$), Calcium/Calmodulin-dependent kinase II ($CaMKII$), and MAP kinase pathways $\rightarrow$ gene transcription (c-Fos, CREB) $\rightarrow$ persistent synaptic strengthening.
  • Characteristics: Heterosynaptic (spreads to adjacent non-stimulated dorsal horn synapses); Long-lasting (hours, days, or permanent).
  • Clinical Signs: Secondary hyperalgesia (increased pain sensitivity in undamaged adjacent tissue) and tactile allodynia ($A\beta$ light touch activates pain pathways via loss of GABAergic/glycinergic inhibition).
  • Glial Activation & Neuroinflammation:
  • Spinal microglia (innate immune cells) and astrocytes become activated following sustained peripheral input or nerve injury.
  • Activated microglia downregulate the potassium-chloride cotransporter $KCC2$ in lamina I projection neurons via BDNF release, flipping the chloride equilibrium potential so that GABA and glycine become depolarizing (excitatory) instead of inhibitory.

3. Pain Classification by Presumed Neurobiological Mechanism (Curriculum 1.1.5)

The EFIC curriculum requires mastery of the mechanism-based triad:

Parameter Nociceptive Pain Neuropathic Pain Nociplastic Pain
IASP Definition Pain that arises from actual or threatened damage to non-neural tissue and is due to the activation of nociceptors. Pain caused by a lesion or disease of the somatosensory nervous system. Pain arising from altered nociception despite no clear evidence of actual or threatened tissue damage causing the activation of peripheral nociceptors or evidence for disease or lesion of the somatosensory system causing the pain.
Typical Clinical Examples Osteoarthritis, acute ligament sprain, post-op surgical incision, rheumatoid flare. Diabetic polyneuropathy, post-herpetic neuralgia, lumbar radiculopathy, trigeminal neuralgia. Fibromyalgia, chronic non-specific low back pain, irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), complex regional pain syndrome Type 1.
Pattern of Aggravation Proportionate mechanical pattern (aggravated by specific loading, eased by unloading). Neuroanatomically plausible distribution (dermatomal, peripheral nerve); provoked by neural tension / compression. Disproportionate, widespread, multifocal; non-mechanical aggravation; provoked by cold, weather, mental stress, sleep loss.
Sensory Findings Localized tenderness; absence of widespread allodynia or neurological deficits. Confirmatory sensory loss (hypoesthesia/hypoalgesia) and/or gain (allodynia, hyperalgesia) strictly within nerve distribution. Widespread bilateral hyperalgesia/allodynia; reduced CPM; heightened temporal summation; pressure pain threshold (PPT) lowered globally.
Associated Features Typically absent unless long-standing. Focal motor weakness, absent deep tendon reflex in nerve root. Severe unrefreshing sleep, chronic fatigue, cognitive fog, sensory hypersensitivity (bright lights, sounds, odors).

Diagnostic Criteria for Nociplastic Pain (Kosek et al. 2021 / EFIC Master Standard):

To classify musculoskeletal pain as nociplastic, the patient must meet: 1. Pain duration $\ge 3\text{ months}$ (chronic). 2. Pain distribution is regional or multifocal / widespread rather than discrete. 3. Pain cannot be entirely explained by identifiable nociceptive or neuropathic mechanisms. 4. Clinical signs of pain hypersensitivity (at least one): - Evoked pain hypersensitivity (dynamic mechanical allodynia, static allodynia, cold/heat allodynia, or pinprick hyperalgesia). 5. History of pain hypersensitivity and presence of comorbid symptoms (at least one): - Extreme sensitivity to sound, light, or odors. - Sleep disturbance with frequent nocturnal awakenings. - Severe fatigue. - Cognitive dysfunction (concentration difficulties, memory lapses).

4. Language of Pain Science & Clinical Classification Systems (Curriculum 1.2)

A. IASP Taxonomy (2020 Definitions)

  • Pain: An unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage. (Note: Pain and nociception are different phenomena; pain cannot be inferred solely from sensory activity).
  • Allodynia: Pain due to a stimulus that does not normally provoke pain.
  • Hyperalgesia: Increased pain from a stimulus that normally provokes pain.
  • Hypoalgesia: Diminished pain in response to a normally painful stimulus.
  • Analgesia: Absence of pain in response to stimulation which would normally be painful.
  • Hyperpathia: A painful syndrome characterized by an abnormally painful reaction to a stimulus, especially a repetitive stimulus, as well as an increased threshold.
  • Dysesthesia: An unpleasant abnormal sensation, whether spontaneous or evoked.
  • Paresthesia: An abnormal sensation, whether spontaneous or evoked (unlike dysesthesia, not unpleasant).
  • Neuropathy: A disturbance of function or pathological change in a nerve.
  • Neuralgia: Pain in the distribution of a nerve or nerves.

B. ICD-11 Chronic Pain Classification (Treede et al. / WHO MG30)

ICD-11 divides chronic pain ($\ge 3\text{ months}$) into: 1. MG30.0 Chronic Primary Pain: - Pain in one or more anatomical regions that persists for $>3$ months and is associated with significant emotional distress or functional disability, and cannot be better explained by another chronic condition. - Subtypes: Chronic widespread pain (Fibromyalgia), Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS), Chronic primary headache/orofacial pain, Chronic primary visceral pain, Chronic primary musculoskeletal pain (e.g., non-specific CLBP). 2. MG30.1–MG30.6 Chronic Secondary Pain (Pain is secondary to an underlying disease entity): - MG30.1 Chronic Cancer-Related Pain (due to tumor progression or cancer therapy). - MG30.2 Chronic Post-Surgical or Post-Traumatic Pain (CPSP). - MG30.3 Chronic Neuropathic Pain (peripheral or central). - MG30.4 Chronic Secondary Headache and Orofacial Pain (e.g., TMD, cervicogenic, giant cell arteritis). - MG30.5 Chronic Secondary Visceral Pain (e.g., Crohn's disease, endometriosis). - MG30.6 Chronic Secondary Musculoskeletal Pain (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, structural spondylolisthesis, OA).

5. Multidimensional Nature of Pain & Lifestyle Factors (Curriculum 1.3)

A. Multidimensional Domains (1.3.1)

  • Sensory-Discriminative: Location, spatial extent, intensity, quality (throbbing, burning, aching).
  • Affective-Motivational: Threat value, fear, unpleasantness, distress, avoidance motivation.
  • Cognitive-Evaluative: Catastrophizing, meaning attributed to pain, self-efficacy, expectation, beliefs about damage.
  • Socio-Cultural: Family dynamics, workplace ergonomics, socioeconomic status, ethnic health disparities, insurance/litigation systems.

B. Lifestyle Factors & Multimorbidity (1.3.1)

  • Sleep: Bidirectional relationship (poor sleep predicts next-day pain more strongly than pain predicts sleep disruption). Sleep fragmentation and loss of slow-wave sleep impairs descending endogenous analgesia (reduces CPM), induces systemic low-grade inflammation ($\text{IL}-6, \text{TNF}-\alpha$), and amplifies central nociceptive sensitivity.
  • EFIC Contemporary Knowledge Benchmark: Poor sleep and pain commonly co-exist across all pain mechanisms (nociceptive, neuropathic, and nociplastic), not just in depression or nociplastic pain. Slaap kan betrouwbaar gemeten worden (ISI, PSQI, actigrafie). Slaapinterventies (zoals CBT-I) verminderen pijn als en slechts als een kritieke drempel van slaapverbetering (critical level of sleep improvement) wordt bereikt; marginale verbetering vermindert pijn niet.
  • Physical Activity: Regular moderate physical activity upregulates endogenous opioid and endocannabinoid tone, increases brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) in the hippocampus, and reduces neuroinflammation. Sedentary lifestyle promotes central sensitization.
  • Nutrition & Metabolic Health: Visceral adiposity releases pro-inflammatory adipokines (leptin, resistin), promoting a systemic pro-inflammatory environment. High glycemic index diets promote advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) sensitizing peripheral receptors.
  • Stress & Allostatic Load: Chronic mental stress causes sustained HPA-axis dysregulation (elevated cortisol followed by glucocorticoid receptor resistance) and autonomic sympathetic hyperactivity, blunting endogenous descending inhibition.
  • Multimorbidity: Pain rarely exists in isolation; presence of comorbid depression, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, or obesity synergistically increases pain disability and chronification risk.

6. Landmark Syntheses from the EFIC Reading List

📄 Paper 1: Malfliet et al. (2017) — Brain Changes & Cognitive-Emotional Factors

  • Citation: Malfliet A, Coppieters I, et al. Eur J Pain, 2017.
  • Systematic Review of 28 MRI Studies:
  • High pain catastrophizing correlates directly with gray matter atrophy in the Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex (DLPFC) ($r = -0.66$). Because the DLPFC normally provides top-down inhibitory control over limbic areas, thinning directly impairs descending pain inhibition.
  • Resting-state fMRI shows hyperconnectivity between medial PFC and the Default Mode Network (DMN), demonstrating perpetual pain rumination during rest.
  • Plasticity: Successful CBT and pain neuroscience education reverse DLPFC gray matter thinning.

📄 Paper 2: Simons, Elman, & Borsook (2014) — Neural Systems in Chronic Pain

  • Citation: Simons LE, Elman I, Borsook D. Neurosci Biobehav Rev, 2014.
  • The 4 Overlapping Neural Circuits:
  • Sensory-Discriminative: $S1, S2$, posterior insula, lateral thalamus.
  • Affective-Motivational / Salience: Anterior Insula, ACC, amygdala, fronto-striatal loops.
  • Cognitive-Evaluative / Executive: Prefrontal cortex (DLPFC, OFC).
  • Reward / Valuation Circuit: Ventral Tegmental Area (VTA), Nucleus Accumbens (NAc). Chronic pain causes an allostatic shift: blunted natural reward (anhedonia) and dominance of pain avoidance.

📄 Paper 3A: Le Bars, Mouraux, & Plaghki (2017) — Peripheral Mechanisms & Psychophysiology

  • Citation: Le Bars D, Mouraux A, Plaghki L. Physiologie de la douleur : aspects psychophysiologiques et mécanismes périphériques. EMC - Anesthésie-Réanimation 2017; [Article 36-020-A-10].
  • Core Scope & Exam Essentials (Official EFIC Mandatory Reading):
  • High-Threshold Transducers: Nociceptors are specialized physiological sensory transducers responding exclusively to actual or potential tissue-damaging stimuli (thermal $>43^\circ\text{C}$, mechanical, chemical), NOT non-specific general receptors pushed to high intensity.
  • First vs. Second Pain Kinetics: Rapid mechanical/thermal pricking pain conveyed by lightly myelinated $A\delta$ fibers ($5\text{–}30\text{ m/s}$) vs. slow, burning, poorly localized second pain conveyed by unmyelinated $C$ fibers ($0.5\text{–}2\text{ m/s}$).
  • Neurogenic Flare & Axon Reflex: Antidromic depolarization of peptidergic $C$-fibers causes peripheral release of CGRP (arteriolar vasodilation / flare) and Substance P (plasma extravasation / wheal/edema) without requiring central spinal activation.
  • Studiegids Verwijzing: Integraal uitgewerkt in Sectie 2.A (Primary Afferents & Peripheral Sensors) hierboven.

📄 Paper 3B: Mouraux, Plaghki, & Le Bars (2017) — Central Mechanisms & Descending Controls

  • Citation: Mouraux A, Plaghki L, Le Bars D. Physiologie de la douleur : mécanismes centraux et contrôles. EMC - Anesthésie-Réanimation 2017; [Article 36-020-A-20].
  • Core Scope & Exam Essentials (Official EFIC Mandatory Reading):
  • Dual Ascending Routing:
    • Neospinothalamic tract: Laminae I & V $\rightarrow$ lateral thalamus (VPL/VPI) $\rightarrow$ primary and secondary somatosensory cortices ($S1, S2$); encodes sensory-discriminative properties (location, intensity, duration).
    • Paleospinothalamic / Spinoreticulothalamic tract: Laminae I, V, VII, VIII $\rightarrow$ medial thalamus, reticular formation, and parabrachial nucleus $\rightarrow$ anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex (ACC); encodes affective-motivational unpleasantness and motor alerting.
  • Wide Dynamic Range (WDR) Neurons (Lamina V): Neurons that receive convergent monosynaptic input from non-noxious $A\beta$ mechanoreceptors and polysynaptic input from $A\delta$ and $C$ fibers. Subject to wind-up via progressive NMDA receptor activation upon repeated C-fiber firing ($0.5\text{–}2\text{ Hz}$).
  • Diffuse Noxious Inhibitory Controls (DNIC): Discovered by Le Bars (1979). A unique spino-bulbo-spinal negative feedback loop: noxious conditioning input anywhere on the body activates the subnucleus reticularis dorsalis (SRD) in the caudal medulla, sending descending non-opioid/noradrenergic inhibitory signals to silence all other dorsal horn WDR neurons outside the conditioning field ("pain inhibits pain").
  • Studiegids Verwijzing: Integraal uitgewerkt in Sectie 2.B (Spinal Architecture) en Sectie 3 (Ascending & Descending Modulation) hierboven.
