Section 2: Principles of Assessment and Measurement — EFIC Ingest & Study Guide

This module synthesizes the clinical frameworks, diagnostic grading criteria, mechanism-based classifications, psychometric measurement tools, and physical examination protocols required for Section Two: Principles of Assessment and Measurement of the official EFIC Core Curriculum.

1. Clinical Frameworks & Diagnostic Algorithms (Curriculum 2.1)

A. WHO International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF)

The EFIC curriculum mandates the application of the ICF model to pain physiotherapy assessment: - Body Functions & Structures ($b/s$): Nociceptive processing, central sensitization, range of motion, muscle strength, joint stability, structural lesions, sleep architecture. - Activities ($d$): Walking tolerance, lifting, bending, sitting tolerance, self-care, stairs, dressing. - Participation ($d$): Paid employment, household roles, recreational pursuits, family life, community engagement. - Environmental Factors ($e$): Physical workplace setup, ergonomic demands, insurance policies, clinical invalidation vs. social support, assistive technology. - Personal Factors: Age, sex/gender, coping style, catastrophizing, fear-avoidance beliefs, pain self-efficacy, health literacy, adverse childhood experiences (ACEs).

B. Neuropathic Pain: Updated Diagnostic Grading System (Finnerup et al. 2016)

Paper: Finnerup NB, Haroutounian S, Kamerman P, Baron R, Bennett DL, et al. Neuropathic pain: an updated grading system for research and clinical practice. Pain, 2016.

The 4-Step Neuropathic Diagnostic Algorithm:

  1. Plausible Neuroanatomical Distribution: Pain distribution is neuroanatomically plausible (e.g., peripheral nerve territory, dermatomal, or central somatotopic distribution).
  2. Relevant History: History suggestive of a relevant lesion or disease affecting the somatosensory nervous system (e.g., stroke, spinal cord injury, herpes zoster, radiculopathy, diabetic polyneuropathy).
  3. Confirmed Negative/Positive Sensory Signs: Clinical sensory examination demonstrates negative signs (sensory loss: hypoesthesia, hypoalgesia) and/or positive signs (allodynia, hyperalgesia) confined strictly to the neuroanatomically plausible area.
  4. Diagnostic Test Confirmation: Objective diagnostic test confirming lesion/disease of somatosensory system (e.g., MRI/CT imaging confirming nerve root compression, EMG/nerve conduction studies confirming axonal loss, skin biopsy showing reduced intraepidermal nerve fiber density).

Grading Levels (Crucial for EFIC MCQs):

  • Possible Neuropathic Pain: Criteria (1) + (2) met (plausible distribution + relevant history). No physical examination or confirmatory tests yet performed.
  • Probable Neuropathic Pain: Criteria (1) + (2) + (3) met (plausible distribution + relevant history + clinical sensory examination confirms signs).
  • Definite Neuropathic Pain: Criteria (1) + (2) + (3) + (4) met (all criteria met including objective diagnostic test).

C. Mechanism-Based Musculoskeletal Pain Classification (Smart et al. 2011)

Paper: Smart KM, Blake C, Staines A, Doody C. The Discriminative validity of "nociceptive," "peripheral neuropathic," and "central sensitization" as mechanisms-based classifications of musculoskeletal pain. Clin J Pain, 2011.

Smart et al. established validated clinical indicator clusters that discriminate between three primary mechanisms:

Mechanism Defining Clinical Features (Clusters)
Nociceptive Pain • Pain localized to area of injury/dysfunction.
• Clear, proportionate mechanical / anatomical pattern of aggravation and easing.
• Intermittent, sharp with movement or dull ache at rest.
• Absence of dysesthesia, burning, or allodynia.
• Pain resolves or fluctuates predictably with physical loading.
Peripheral Neuropathic Pain • Pain referred in a dermatomal or cutaneous nerve distribution.
• History of nerve injury, pathology, or mechanical compromise.
• Pain provocable with neurodynamic tests (SLR, ULNT) and nerve trunk palpation.
• Positive symptoms: burning, shooting, electric shocks, hyperalgesia.
• Negative symptoms: numbness, sensory loss, motor weakness/reflex changes.
Central Sensitization Pain • Disproportionate, non-mechanical, unpredictable pattern of pain aggravation.
• Widespread, diffuse pain distribution not conforming to anatomical pathways.
• Hyperalgesia and/or allodynia across broad areas (secondary hyperalgesia).
• Presence of psychosocial symptoms: severe fatigue, unrefreshing sleep, cognitive difficulties, sensory hypersensitivity (bright lights, noise, odors, stress).
• High scores on the Central Sensitization Inventory (CSI $\ge 40/100$).

D. Neurodynamic Testing & Structural Differentiation

  • Straight Leg Raise (SLR) Mechanics: Stresses the sciatic nerve, lumbosacral nerve roots (L4–S2), and dura mater.
  • Sensitizing Maneuvers (Increase Neural Strain):
    • Ankle dorsiflexion + eversion (Bragard's test / tibial nerve bias).
    • Ankle plantarflexion + inversion (fibular / common peroneal nerve bias).
    • Ankle dorsiflexion + inversion (sural nerve bias).
    • Hip adduction and hip medial (internal) rotation.
    • Cervical and trunk flexion (Slump / Soto-Hall maneuver).
  • Desensitizing / Slackening Maneuvers (Decrease Neural Strain):
    • Knee flexion: Completely slacks the sciatic nerve. If distal radiating leg symptoms immediately abate when the knee is slightly flexed, it confirms neurogenic rather than muscular (hamstring) origin.
    • Ankle neutral/plantarflexion; hip external rotation and abduction.
  • Upper Limb Neurodynamic Tests (ULNT):
    • ULNT1 (Median nerve bias): Shoulder depression, abduction to $110^\circ$, wrist/finger extension, forearm supination, shoulder external rotation, elbow extension. Sensitizing: contralateral cervical lateral flexion.
    • ULNT2A (Median nerve bias - alternative): Scapular depression, elbow extension, lateral rotation, wrist extension.
    • ULNT2B (Radial nerve bias): Scapular depression, elbow extension, shoulder internal rotation, forearm pronation, wrist flexion + ulnar deviation.
    • ULNT3 (Ulnar nerve bias): Wrist extension + radial deviation, forearm pronation, elbow flexion, shoulder external rotation, shoulder depression, abduction to $90^\circ$ ("waiter's hand").

E. Clinical Assessment of Specific Regional Pain Conditions (Curriculum 2.1 & 4.2)

1. Headache and Orofacial Pain:

  • Cervicogenic Headache (CGH - IHS / Sjaastad Criteria):
    • Unilateral headache without side shift, radiating from occipital region to fronto-temporal areas.
    • Aggravated by specific neck movements or sustained awkward postures.
    • Provoked by manual palpation of ipsilateral upper cervical facet joints ($C1\text{–}C3$).
    • Flexion-Rotation Test (FRT): High diagnostic accuracy (sensitivity 90%, specificity 90%). Normal rotation in full cervical flexion is $\approx 44^\circ$ to each side. A restriction of $\ge 10^\circ$ to the symptomatic side indicates $C1\text{–}C2$ dysfunction.
  • Temporomandibular Disorders (DC-TMD Criteria):
    • Pain in jaw, temple, in front of the ear, or inside the ear.
    • Aggravated by jaw motion (chewing, yawning, speaking).
    • Replication of familiar pain on palpation of masseter or temporalis muscles (myofascial TMD) or temporomandibular joint capsule (arthralgia).
    • Joint sounds (clicking = disc displacement with reduction; crepitus = osteoarthritis / degenerative joint disease).

2. Chronic Pelvic Pain (CPPS, Endometriosis, Vulvodynia):

  • Assessment Components:
    • Screen for red flags (unexplained hematuria, rectal bleeding, post-menopausal bleeding, pelvic masses).
    • Visceral-somatic convergence: Chronic visceral irritation (endometriosis, interstitial cystitis) triggers secondary pelvic floor hypertonicity, spasm, and cutaneous allodynia over T10–S4 dermatomes.
    • Musculoskeletal assessment: Lumbopelvic stability, hip mobility, external pelvic palpation (obturator internus, pubic symphysis, sacroiliac joints), and gentle internal myofascial assessment (when certified).
    • Trauma-informed care: High prevalence of psychological trauma, sexual abuse history, or medical trauma; mandatory explicit consent and patient control over examination.

3. Visceral Pain & Somatic Referral:

  • Characterized by dull, diffuse, poorly localized aching often accompanied by autonomic responses (nausea, diaphoresis, pallor, bradycardia/tachycardia).
  • Mediated by convergent visceral and somatic afferents onto wide dynamic range (WDR) neurons in lamina V of the dorsal horn.
  • Elicits secondary somatic hyperalgesia and muscle guarding in corresponding dermatomes/myotomes (e.g., gallbladder referring to right shoulder / C3-C5 via phrenic nerve; cardiac ischemia referring to left inner arm / T1-T2).

F. Health Literacy, Stigma & Diagnostic Uncertainty (Curriculum 1.2.2 & 2.1.4)

  • Health Literacy Principles:
    • Dynamic and modifiable; health literacy is a bidirectional clinical property, not just a patient deficit.
    • Teach-Back Method: Asking the patient to summarize the management plan in their own words; identifies cognitive gaps without patronizing.
    • Use of plain language: Replace catastrophic biomedical framing ("bone-on-bone", "crumbling spine", "slipped disc") with reassuring biopsychosocial metaphors ("sensitive car alarm", "protective muscle splinting", "robust joint resilience").
  • Navigating Diagnostic Uncertainty (When Tests are Normal):
    • Validating the reality of the patient's suffering ("Your pain is 100% real").
    • Explaining the limitation of structural imaging ("Scans show anatomy, like a photograph, but they cannot show how the volume control of the nervous system is set").
    • De-stigmatizing nociplastic pain by validating neurobiological changes (central sensitization, cortical reorganization) without resorting to psychogenic dismissal.

2. Ingested Evidence Syntheses: Measurement & Psychometrics (Curriculum 2.2)

📄 Paper 4: Fillingim, Loeser, Baron, & Edwards (2016) — Assessment Domains & Methods

Citation: Fillingim RB, Loeser JD, Baron R, Edwards RR. Assessment of Chronic Pain: Domains, Methods, and Mechanisms. J Pain. 2016 Sep;17(9 Suppl):T10-26.

  • The IMMPACT / ACTTION Core Outcome Domains for Chronic Pain:
    1. Pain Intensity: Measured dynamically (at rest, on movement, worst, average over 7 days).
    2. Physical Functioning: Multidimensional assessment of mobility, daily activities, role functioning.
    3. Emotional Functioning: Depressive symptoms, anxiety, anger, pain catastrophizing.
    4. Participant Ratings of Global Improvement: Patient Global Impression of Change (PGIC) (7-point Likert scale).
    5. Symptoms and Adverse Events: Treatment tolerability, side effects.
    6. Participant Disposition: Adherence, study completion/dropouts.
  • Quantitative Sensory Testing (QST - DFNS Protocol):
    • Standardized somatosensory phenotyping evaluating thermal thresholds (CDT, WDT, TSL, CPT, HPT), mechanical detection (MDT via von Frey filaments), mechanical pain threshold (MPT via pinprick), mechanical pain sensitivity (MPS), dynamic mechanical allodynia (DMA via brush), wind-up ratio (WUR), and vibration detection (VDT via tuning fork).
    • Detects sensory loss (neuropathic axonal injury) vs. sensory gain (hyperalgesia/allodynia indicating peripheral or central sensitization).

📄 Paper 5: Faculty of Pain Medicine (FPM) & British Pain Society (BPS) 2019 — Outcome Measures

Citation: Faculty of Pain Medicine & British Pain Society. Outcome Measures. Royal College of Anaesthetists, 2019.

  • Core Minimum Dataset for Chronic Pain Audit:
    • Pain Severity & Interference: Brief Pain Inventory (BPI). Assesses pain intensity (4 items) and interference with 7 life domains (general activity, mood, walking ability, normal work, relationships, sleep, enjoyment of life).
    • Health-Related Quality of Life: EQ-5D (EuroQol 5 Dimensions: Mobility, Self-Care, Usual Activities, Pain/Discomfort, Anxiety/Depression + EQ-VAS 0–100).
    • Psychological Distress: HADS (Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale) or PHQ-9 (depression) / GAD-7 (anxiety).
    • Pain Self-Efficacy: PSEQ (Pain Self-Efficacy Questionnaire): 10 items, scored 0–60. Higher scores ($>40$) reflect strong confidence in performing activities despite pain; scores $<30$ predict poor engagement and high disability.
    • Kinesiophobia: TSK (Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia): 17 items, score 17–68; scores $>37$ indicate high fear of movement.
    • Catastrophizing: PCS (Pain Catastrophizing Scale): 13 items, score 0–52; scores $\ge 30$ indicate clinically relevant catastrophizing (subscales: Rumination, Magnification, Helplessness).

📄 Paper 6: Wang et al. (2016) — Persistent Post-Surgical Pain Predictors

Citation: Wang L, Guyatt GH, Kennedy SA, et al. Predictors of persistent pain after breast cancer surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies. CMAJ. 2016;188(14):E352-E361.

  • Quantitative Predictors of Chronification (Persistent Post-Surgical Pain / PPSP):
    1. Axillary Lymph Node Dissection (ALND): Strongest independent surgical predictor ($\text{OR} = 2.41$, 95% CI 1.73–3.35) due to intercostobrachial nerve damage. Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) carries significantly lower risk.
    2. Younger Age: Each decade decrease in age increases PPSP risk ($\text{OR} = 1.36$).
    3. Radiotherapy: Significantly increases risk ($\text{OR} = 1.35$) via chronic microvascular ischemia and radiation fibrosis.
    4. Acute Post-Operative Pain: Severe acute pain within 24–72 hours post-surgery increases risk ($\text{OR} = 1.16\text{ per }1\text{-point increase}$ on 0–10 scale).
    5. Preoperative Psychological Factors: High catastrophizing, state anxiety, and depression.
    6. Surgical Scope: In contrast to ALND, the extent of breast tissue resection (mastectomy vs. breast-conserving lumpectomy) and adjuvant chemotherapy showed NO significant association with PPSP risk.

📄 Paper 7: Verbunt, Huijnen, & Seelen (2012) — Objective Activity Monitoring

Citation: Verbunt JA, Huijnen IP, Seelen HA. Assessment of physical activity by movement registration systems in chronic pain: methodological considerations. Clin J Pain. 2012;28(6):496-504.

  • Central Findings:
    1. Subjective vs. Objective Divergence: Patient self-reported physical activity (diaries/questionnaires) correlates poorly with objective movement registration systems (accelerometers, pedometers). Self-reports reflect pain severity and emotional distress rather than actual movement quantity.
    2. Activity Subgroups in Chronic Pain:
      • Avoiders: Low physical activity, fearful of movement, leading to deconditioning.
      • Persisters / Over-activity (Boom-Bust): High intensity bursts on good days followed by prolonged flare-ups and immobility.
    3. Methodological Requirements: Accelerometry requires multi-day monitoring (at least 4–7 consecutive days, including weekdays and weekends) to reliably capture habitual physical activity patterns.

📄 Paper 8: Tyson & Brown (2014) — Pain Measurement in Neurological Conditions

Citation: Tyson SF, Brown P. How to measure pain in neurological conditions? A systematic review of psychometric properties and clinical utility of measurement tools. Clin Rehabil. 2014;28(7):669-86.

  • Central Findings:
    1. Validated Tools in Stroke, MS, Parkinson's, Spinal Cord Injury:
      • Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) are reliable for unidimensional pain intensity, but the NRS is clinically superior and has lower error rates in cognitively intact patients.
      • Cognitive / Communication Impairment Rule:
        • Right-hemisphere stroke / spatial neglect: Use Verbal Rating Scales (VRS).
        • Left-hemisphere stroke / expressive aphasia: Use Visual / Numeric Scales (NRS / VAS / FPS-R).
    2. Neuropathic Screening Tools:
      • DN4 (Douleur Neuropathique 4): 10 items (4 examination, 6 interview questions; cut-off $\ge 4/10$ indicates neuropathic pain).
      • LANSS (Leeds Assessment of Neuropathic Symptoms and Signs): Cut-off $\ge 12/24$.
      • PainDETECT: Specifically validated for radiculopathy and mixed neuropathic-MSK conditions.

📄 Paper 9: Gordon (2015) — Acute Pain Assessment Beyond Simple Ratings

Citation: Gordon DB. Acute pain assessment tools: let us move beyond simple pain ratings. Curr Opin Anaesthesiol. 2015;28(5):565-9.

  • Key Takeaways for MCQ:
    1. Limitations of Unidimensional Scales: Relying solely on 0–10 NRS at rest leads to over-reliance on opioids, ignores dynamic functional impairment, and fails to evaluate recovery.
    2. Functional Pain Assessment: Pain MUST be assessed during functional activities (e.g., deep breathing, coughing, turning in bed, walking) rather than static resting pain alone.
    3. Clinically Meaningful Difference (MCID): A reduction of $\ge 2$ points or $\ge 30\%$ on the 0–10 NRS represents a clinically important difference in acute and chronic pain.
    4. Multi-domain Acute Assessment: Encompasses: (1) pain intensity on movement, (2) interference with functional goals, (3) adverse medication effects, and (4) patient safety.
