Section 2: Principles of Assessment and Measurement — EFIC Ingest & Study Guide

This module synthesizes the clinical frameworks, diagnostic grading criteria, mechanism-based classifications, and psychometric measurement tools required for Section Two of the EFIC Core Curriculum.

1. Clinical Frameworks & Diagnostic Algorithms

A. WHO International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF)

The EFIC curriculum mandates the application of the ICF model to pain physiotherapy assessment. - Body Functions & Structures ($b/s$): Nociception, central sensitization, range of motion, muscle strength, tissue pathology, sleep architecture. - Activities ($d$): Walking, lifting, bending, sitting tolerance, self-care. - Participation ($d$): Employment, family roles, leisure, community engagement. - Environmental Factors ($e$): Physical workplace setup, ergonomic demands, health policy, medication access, family support vs. invalidation. - Personal Factors: Age, coping style, catastrophizing, fear-avoidance, self-efficacy, health literacy, trauma history.

B. Neuropathic Pain: Updated Grading System

Paper: Finnerup NB, Haroutounian S, Kamerman P, Baron R, Bennett DL, et al. (2016). Neuropathic pain: an updated grading system for research and clinical practice. Pain, 157(8):1599-606. DOI: 10.1097/j.pain.0000000000000492.

The Neuropathic Grading Algorithm:

  1. Plausible Neuroanatomical Distribution: Pain distribution is neuroanatomically plausible (dermatomal, peripheral nerve distribution, or central somatotopic).
  2. Relevant History: History suggestive of a relevant lesion or disease affecting the somatosensory nervous system (e.g., stroke, herpes zoster, spinal trauma, diabetes, nerve compression).
  3. Confirmed Negative/Positive Sensory Signs: Confirmatory sensory examination showing negative signs (sensory loss: hypoesthesia, hypoalgesia) and/or positive signs (hyperalgesia, allodynia) confined to the neuroanatomically plausible area.
  4. Diagnostic Test Confirmation: Objective diagnostic test confirming lesion/disease of somatosensory system (e.g., MRI/CT imaging, nerve conduction studies, EMG, skin biopsy showing reduced intraepidermal nerve fiber density).

Grading Levels (Crucial for MCQs):

  • Possible Neuropathic Pain: Criteria (1) + (2) met (plausible distribution + relevant history). No examination/confirmatory tests yet performed.
  • Probable Neuropathic Pain: Criteria (1) + (2) + (3) met (plausible distribution + relevant history + clinical sensory examination confirms signs).
  • Definite Neuropathic Pain: Criteria (1) + (2) + (3) + (4) met (all criteria met including objective diagnostic test).

C. Mechanism-Based Musculoskeletal Pain Classification

Paper: Smart KM, Blake C, Doody C. (2011). The Discriminative validity of "nociceptive," "peripheral neuropathic," and "central sensitization" as mechanisms-based classifications of musculoskeletal pain. Clin J Pain, 27(8):655-63. DOI: 10.1097/AJP.0b013e318215f16a.

Smart et al. identified distinct clinical indicator clusters that discriminate between three primary mechanisms:

Mechanism Defining Clinical Features (Clusters)
Nociceptive Pain • Pain localized to area of injury/dysfunction.
• Clear, proportionate mechanical / anatomical pattern of aggravation and easing.
• Intermittent, sharp with movement or dull ache at rest.
• Absence of dysesthesia, burning, or allodynia.
• Pain resolves or fluctuates predictably with physical loading.
Peripheral Neuropathic Pain • Pain referred in a dermatomal or cutaneous nerve distribution.
• History of nerve injury, pathology, or mechanical compromise.
• Pain provocable with neurodynamic tests (SLR, ULNT) and nerve palpation.
• Positive symptoms: burning, shooting, electric shocks, hyperalgesia.
• Negative symptoms: numbness, sensory loss, weakness/reflex changes.
Central Sensitization Pain • Disproportionate, non-mechanical, unpredictable pattern of pain aggravation.
• Widespread, diffuse pain distribution not conforming to anatomical pathways.
• Hyperalgesia and/or allodynia across broad areas (secondary hyperalgesia).
• Presence of psychosocial symptoms: severe fatigue, unrefreshing sleep, cognitive difficulties, sensory hypersensitivity (bright lights, noise, odors, stress).
• High scores on the Central Sensitization Inventory (CSI).

D. Neurodynamic Testing & Structural Differentiation (SLR)

  • Straight Leg Raise (SLR) Mechanics: Stresses the sciatic nerve, lumbosacral nerve roots (L4–S2), and dura.
  • Sensitizing Maneuvers (Increase Neural Strain):
    • Ankle dorsiflexion + eversion (Bragard's test / tibial nerve bias).
    • Ankle plantarflexion + inversion (fibular / peroneal nerve bias).
    • Hip adduction.
    • Hip medial (internal) rotation.
    • Neck/trunk flexion (Slump / Soto-Hall maneuver).
  • Desensitizing / Slackening Maneuvers (Decrease Neural Strain):
    • Knee flexion: Completely slacks the sciatic nerve. If symptoms immediately abate when the knee is slightly bent, it confirms neurogenic rather than muscular (hamstring) origin.
    • Ankle neutral/plantarflexion.
    • Hip external rotation / abduction.

E. Health Literacy & Clinical Communication (2.1.4 & 3.1.3)

  • Core Principles: Health literacy is dynamic and modifiable across all phases of care, including chronic conditions. Both the healthcare provider and the patient share responsibility.
  • Evidence-Based Strategies:
    • Using 'Plain English' / plain language (avoiding jargon, unhelpful anatomical catastrophizing).
    • Teach-Back Method: Asking the patient to explain back in their own words what they have understood, verifying comprehension.
  • Yellow Flags & Prognostic Screening (STarT Back / Örebro):
    • Items predicting chronicity: high acute pain, fear-avoidance beliefs ("not safe to be physically active"), catastrophizing/rumination ("worrying thoughts going through my mind"), depressive mood/anhedonia ("not enjoying things").
    • Protective factor / predicts recovery: High positive recovery expectations ("I feel my pain is benign and will be better soon").

2. Ingested Evidence Syntheses: Measurement & Psychometrics

📄 Paper 3: Wang et al. (2016) — Persistent Post-Surgical Pain Predictors

Citation: Wang L, Guyatt GH, Kennedy SA, et al. Predictors of persistent pain after breast cancer surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies. CMAJ. 2016;188(14):E352-E361. DOI: 10.1503/cmaj.151274.

  • Central Findings & Risk Factors:
    1. Younger Age: Younger patients are at significantly higher risk of persistent post-surgical pain (PPSP) (Odds Ratio approx 1.3 to 1.7).
    2. Preoperative Pain & High Acute Postoperative Pain Intensity: High acute pain intensity within the first 24–72 hours post-surgery is one of the strongest independent predictors of chronification.
    3. Psychological Factors: Preoperative catastrophizing, depression, and high state/trait anxiety.
    4. Surgical Factors: Axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) carries a substantially higher risk of PPSP and neuropathic pain compared to sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB).
    5. Adjuvant Therapy: Radiotherapy and chemotherapy increase the risk of chronic persistent neuropathic and musculoskeletal pain.

📄 Paper 4: Verbunt, Huijnen, & Seelen (2012) — Objective Activity Monitoring

Citation: Verbunt JA, Huijnen IP, Seelen HA. Assessment of physical activity by movement registration systems in chronic pain: methodological considerations. Clin J Pain. 2012 Jul;28(6):496-504. DOI: 10.1097/AJP.0b013e318231362e.

  • Central Findings:
    1. Self-Report vs. Objective Monitoring: Patient self-reported physical activity (questionnaires/diaries) correlates poorly with objective movement registration systems (accelerometers, pedometers). Self-reports reflect pain severity and emotional distress rather than actual movement quantity.
    2. Activity Subgroups in Chronic Pain:
      • Avoiders: Low physical activity, fearful of movement, leading to deconditioning.
      • Persisters / Over-activity (Boom-Bust): High intensity bursts followed by prolonged flare-ups and immobility.
    3. Methodological Requirements: Accelerometry requires multi-day monitoring (at least 4–7 consecutive days) including weekends to reliably capture habitual physical activity patterns.

📄 Paper 5: Tyson & Brown (2014) — Pain Measurement in Neurological Conditions

Citation: Tyson SF, Brown P. How to measure pain in neurological conditions? A systematic review of psychometric properties and clinical utility of measurement tools. Clin Rehabil. 2014;28(7):669-86. DOI: 10.1177/0269215513510583.

  • Central Findings:
    1. Validated Tools in Stroke, MS, Parkinson's, Spinal Cord Injury:
      • Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) are reliable for unidimensional pain intensity, but the NRS is clinically superior and has lower error rates in cognitively intact patients.
      • Verbal Rating Scale (VRS) and Faces Pain Scale (FPS-R) are preferred in patients with cognitive impairment, communicative deficits, or post-stroke aphasia.
    2. Neuropathic Screening Tools:
      • DN4 (Douleur Neuropathique 4): 10 items (4 examination, 6 interview questions; cut-off $\ge 4/10$ indicates neuropathic pain). Highly validated across neurological cohorts.
      • LANSS (Leeds Assessment of Neuropathic Symptoms and Signs): Cut-off $\ge 12/24$.
      • PainDETECT: Specifically validated for radiculopathy and mixed neuropathic-MSK conditions.

📄 Paper 6: Gordon (2015) — Acute Pain Assessment Beyond Simple Ratings

Citation: Gordon DB. Acute pain assessment tools: let us move beyond simple pain ratings. Curr Opin Anaesthesiol. 2015 Oct;28(5):565-9. DOI: 10.1097/ACO.0000000000000230.

  • Key Takeaways for MCQ:
    1. Limitations of Unidimensional Scales: Relying solely on 0–10 NRS at rest leads to over-reliance on opioids, ignores dynamic functional impairment, and fails to evaluate recovery.
    2. Functional Pain Assessment: Pain MUST be assessed during functional activities (e.g., deep breathing, coughing, turning in bed, walking) rather than static resting pain alone.
    3. Clinically Meaningful Difference: A reduction of $\ge 2$ points or $\ge 30\%$ on the 0–10 NRS represents a clinically important difference (MCID) in acute and chronic pain.
    4. Multi-domain Acute Assessment: Should encompass: (1) pain intensity on movement, (2) interference with functional goals, (3) adverse medication effects, and (4) patient satisfaction/safety.
