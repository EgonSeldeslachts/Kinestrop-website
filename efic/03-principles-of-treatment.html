Section 3: Principles of Treatment — EFIC Ingest & Study Guide

This module covers the core clinical interventions, Cochrane review evidence bases, mechanism-based therapies, exercise neurobiology, digital health management, and multidisciplinary strategies for Section Three: Principles of Treatment of the official EFIC Core Curriculum.

1. Communication, Contextual Factors & Education (Curriculum 3.1, 3.2, 3.3, 3.4)

A. Contextual Factors, Placebo & Nocebo in Physiotherapy (3.2 & 3.3)

Paper: Testa M, Rossettini G. Enhance placebo, avoid nocebo: How contextual factors affect physiotherapy outcomes. Man Ther. 2016 Aug;24:65-74.

  • The 5 Clusters of Contextual Factors (CFs) in Physiotherapy:
    1. Physiotherapist Characteristics: Professional dress, demonstrated clinical competence, warm empathetic tone, non-verbal open posture, eye contact, gender concordance/preference.
    2. Patient Characteristics: Positive treatment expectations, optimism, prior treatment experiences, internal locus of control.
    3. Patient-Physiotherapist Relationship (Therapeutic Alliance): Shared decision making, deep listening, genuine empathy, therapeutic touch, validation of suffering.
    4. Treatment Characteristics: Rituals of manual examination, visible modern therapeutic equipment, overt diagnostic labeling, convincing therapeutic narrative.
    5. Healthcare Environment: Clean, calm, aesthetically welcoming clinic space, private treatment rooms, clear directional signage.
  • Clinical & Ethical Application:
    • Contextual factors are not noise — they are active, neurobiologically mediated drivers of clinical outcomes ($g \approx 0.5\text{–}0.7$).
    • Placebo Mechanisms: Mediated by prefrontal expectation, release of endogenous opioids and endocannabinoids, and suppression of the limbic salience network (ACC/insula).
    • Nocebo Triggers to Eliminate: Avoid alarming biomechanical language ("your spine is wearing out", "bone-on-bone", "your pelvis is crooked"), dismissive communication, or indifferent body language.

B. Nocebo Hyperalgesia Meta-Analysis (Petersen et al. 2014)

Paper: Petersen GL, Finnerup NB, Colloca L, et al. The magnitude of nocebo effects in pain: a meta-analysis. Pain. 2014 Aug;155(8):1426-34. - Core Findings: - Nocebo hyperalgesia has a moderate-to-large effect size (Hedges' $g = 0.63$). - Negative verbal suggestions paired with conditioning generate the largest effects ($g = 0.77$), but negative verbal suggestion alone induces significant hyperalgesia ($g = 0.52$). - Neurochemical Mechanism: Driven by anxiety and the cholecystokinin (CCK) system; completely reversible with the CCK antagonist proglumide.

C. Pain Neuroscience Education (PNE) & Patient Education (3.4)

  • Moseley & Butler (2015) — 15 Years of Explaining Pain:
    • PNE reconceptualizes pain: shifts patient beliefs from pain as a marker of tissue pathology to pain as a marker of perceived danger/threat.
    • PNE reduces pain catastrophizing, kinesiophobia, healthcare overutilization, and sick leave.
    • Clinical Rule: Standalone PNE improves beliefs and catastrophizing, but must be combined with active movement / physical exercise to significantly reduce physical disability and restore function.
  • Geneen et al. (2015) Systematic Review: Pain biology education achieves moderate increases in pain knowledge ($\text{SMD} = 0.54$) and reductions in catastrophizing ($\text{SMD} = -0.37$).
  • Engers et al. (2008) Cochrane Review on LBP Education:
    • Acute LBP: Brief patient education (e.g., The Back Book, reassurance that back pain is common and benign, advice to stay active) reduces sick leave and primary care consultations.
    • Chronic LBP: Brief generic advice is ineffective; requires comprehensive, individualized biopsychosocial education.

2. Behavioural & Psychological Interventions (Curriculum 3.5)

A. The "Decade of the Flags" Framework (Nicholas et al. 2011)

Citation: Nicholas MK, Linton SJ, Watson PJ, Main CJ. Early identification and management of psychological risk factors ("yellow flags") in patients with low back pain: a reappraisal. Phys Ther. 2011 May;91(5):737-53.

Flag Category Focus & Definition Clinical Examples Clinical Management Strategy
Red Flags Serious biomedical pathology Cauda equina, fracture, malignancy, infection, cord compression Urgent medical / surgical referral
Orange Flags Psychiatric pathology Major clinical depression, severe anxiety, personality disorder, active psychosis Psychiatric / psychological referral
Yellow Flags Psychological beliefs & cognitive appraisals Pain catastrophizing, fear-avoidance beliefs, hypervigilance, passive coping PNE, CBT-informed physiotherapy, graded exposure
Blue Flags Social & workplace perceptions Perceived high job stress, low supervisor support, low peer support, fear of re-injury at work Workplace ergonomics, modified duty negotiation, employer communication
Black Flags Systemic & institutional barriers Heavy manual labor with no light duty, contentious insurance claims, legal disputes Occupational health, social work, legal resolution

B. Graded Activity vs. Graded Exposure (Macedo et al. 2010 & Linton et al. 2018)

  • Macedo et al. (2010) Systematic Review:
    • Graded Activity (Operant Conditioning / Fordyce):
      • Focuses on functional quota setting.
      • Progression is strictly time-contingent, NOT pain-contingent.
      • Extinguishes pain behaviors (grimacing, moaning, resting) by withholding attention, while positively reinforcing healthy activity quotas.
    • Graded Exposure (In Vivo Cognitive-Behavioral Exposure / Vlaeyen):
      • Focuses on expectational threat violation.
      • Uses the PHODA (Photographs of Daily Activities) or fear hierarchy to identify specifically feared movements (e.g., lifting with a rounded spine).
      • Patients engage in the feared movement in a graded manner to disconfirm catastrophic expectations ("I predicted my spine would snap, but I bent forward and it did not").
    • Comparative Outcome: Graded exposure produces greater reductions in catastrophizing and kinesiophobia in highly fearful patients.
  • Linton, Flink, & Vlaeyen (2018) — Psychological Etiology of Pain:
    • Pain behaviors develop through Pavlovian respondent conditioning (innocuous proprioceptive sensations become conditioned stimuli eliciting fear) and operant conditioning.
    • Avoidance acts as a safety behavior: While avoidance temporarily relieves fear (negative reinforcement), it prevents the extinction of threat expectations and guarantees physical deconditioning.

C. Acceptance and Mindfulness-Based Interventions (Veehof et al. 2016)

Paper: Veehof MM, Oskam M, Schreurs KM, Bohlmeijer ET. Acceptance- and mindfulness-based interventions for the treatment of chronic pain: a meta-analytic review. Cogn Behav Ther. 2016;45(1):5-31. - Evidence Quality: Low-to-high quality across 25 RCTs ($N=1,285$). - Effect Sizes by Outcome: - Moderate effect: Specifically on pain interference ($\text{SMD} = 0.62$) and anxiety ($\text{SMD} = 0.51$). - Small effect: On pain intensity ($\text{SMD} = 0.37$), depression ($\text{SMD} = 0.35$), and disability ($\text{SMD} = 0.31$). - Core Principle: ACT focuses on psychological flexibility and value-based living in the presence of pain, rather than trying to eliminate pain sensations.

3. Physical Activity & Exercise Neurobiology (Curriculum 3.6)

A. Central Mechanisms of Exercise-Induced Analgesia & Hyperalgesia

Paper: Lima LV, Abner TSS, Sluka KA. Does exercise increase or decrease pain? Central mechanisms underlying these two phenomena. J Physiol. 2017 Jul 1;595(13):4141-4150. Paper: Sluka KA, Frey-Law L, Hoeger Bement M. Exercise-induced pain and analgesia? Underlying mechanisms and clinical translation. Pain. 2018 Sep;159 Suppl 1:S91-S97.

  • Exercise-Induced Hypoalgesia (EIH) in Healthy Individuals:
    • Acute bouts of exercise increase pain thresholds locally and systemically.
    • Mechanisms:
      1. Endogenous Opioidergic System: Upregulation of $\mu$-opioid receptor activation in the Rostroventromedial Medulla (RVM) and Periaqueductal Gray (PAG). Blocked by naloxone.
      2. Endocannabinoid System: Circulating anandamide and 2-AG levels increase during sustained aerobic exercise ($>60\%\ \text{VO}_2\text{max}$), acting via $CB_1$ receptors.
      3. Serotonergic and Noradrenergic Descending Inhibition: Increases dorsal horn serotonin ($5\text{-HT}_1A$) and noradrenaline ($\alpha_2$-adrenergic receptors).
      4. Anti-inflammatory Cytokines: Contracting skeletal muscle releases "myokines" (particularly $\text{IL}-6$, which stimulates anti-inflammatory $\text{IL}-10$ and $\text{IL}-1\text{ra}$ and inhibits $\text{TNF}-\alpha$).
  • Exercise-Induced Hyperalgesia in Chronic / Nociplastic Pain:
    • Patients with fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome, and severe central sensitization often experience a paradoxical worsening of pain following exercise.
    • Why?
      1. Impaired descending inhibitory pathways (deficient CPM / DNIC).
      2. Hyperactive descending facilitation (amplified ON-cells in RVM).
      3. Unaccustomed eccentric muscle damage releases protons, ATP, and NGF, activating already sensitized primary afferents.
      4. Lack of normal exercise-induced mobilization of endogenous opioids.
  • Clinical Prescription Guidelines for Nociplastic Pain (Sluka 2018):
    • Avoid high-intensity, unaccustomed eccentric loading at initial presentation.
    • Begin with low-intensity, sustained aerobic exercise (walking, cycling, aquatic exercise) or short-duration submaximal isometric contractions ($20\text{–}25\%$ MVC for 3–5 minutes).
    • Use time-contingent pacing and gradual progression to restore endogenous opioid sensitivity without triggering post-exertional flares.

B. Clinical Evidence Across Exercise Modalities

  • Geneen et al. (2017) Cochrane Overview: Physical activity and exercise produce consistent, statistically significant reductions in pain and disability across all chronic musculoskeletal conditions; safe, low adverse event profile.
  • Bidonde et al. (2017) Cochrane Review (Fibromyalgia): Aerobic exercise training improves health-related quality of life ($\text{MD} = -5.6/100$), physical function ($\text{MD} = -6.4/100$), and pain ($\text{MD} = -8.8/100$) with moderate-quality evidence.
  • Searle et al. (2015) Meta-Analysis (CLBP): Motor control exercise ($\text{SMD} = -0.49$) and resistance training ($\text{SMD} = -0.45$) show slightly superior short-term pain relief, but long-term functional equivalence across all active exercise modalities.

4. Digital Health Management (Curriculum 3.7)

A. Efficacy of Internet-Delivered Interventions

  • Eccleston et al. (2014) Cochrane Review — Internet-Delivered Psychological Therapies for Chronic Pain:
    • Evaluated 15 RCTs ($N=2,012$) in adults.
    • Disability: Moderate reduction at post-treatment ($\text{SMD} = -0.50$, 95% CI -0.79 to -0.20).
    • Pain Intensity: Small reduction ($\text{SMD} = -0.37$, 95% CI -0.59 to -0.15).
    • Depression & Anxiety: Small-to-moderate improvements.
    • Critical Finding: Therapist-guided internet interventions are substantially more effective than unguided, automated self-help programs.

B. Wearables & Electronic Activity Feedback

  • Oliveira et al. (2018) Systematic Review & Meta-Analysis (Arch Phys Med Rehabil):
    • Citation: Oliveira CB, Franco MR, Maher CG, et al. Physical Activity-Based Interventions Using Electronic Feedback May Be Ineffective in Reducing Pain and Disability in Patients With Chronic Musculoskeletal Pain. Arch Phys Med Rehabil. 2018 Sep;99(9):1900-1912.
    • Crucial MCQ Finding: Providing patients with electronic activity feedback alone (wearable pedometers, smartphone step counters, smartwatch alerts) does NOT significantly reduce pain or disability compared to minimal interventions.
    • Mechanism: Merely quantifying daily steps or activity without personalized behavioral counseling, biopsychosocial goal-setting, and pacing fails to alter pain beliefs or functional coping.

C. Telehealth & Virtual Reality (VR)

  • Telehealth in Pain Physiotherapy: Effective for delivering exercise coaching, PNE, and remote monitoring; matches clinic-based outcomes for chronic low back and osteoarthritis pain when high therapeutic alliance is maintained.
  • Immersive Virtual Reality (VR): Acts via intense attentional distraction (reducing sensory-discriminative and insular BOLD signaling) and sensorimotor recalibration (providing visual illusion of painless limb excursion). Effective for acute procedural pain, burns, and CRPS motor retraining.

A. High-Yield Evidence Summary of Passive Modalities

Spinal Manipulation (SMT) Rubinstein et al. (2011) Cochrane / Spine ADJUNCTIVE / EQUIVALENT. Provides small short-term pain relief and functional improvement; equivalent to exercise and GP care, NOT clinically superior.

B. Complementary & Active Self-Care Therapies (Curriculum 3.10)

  • Wieland et al. (2017) Cochrane Review — Yoga for Chronic Non-Specific LBP:
    • Low-to-moderate quality evidence that yoga produces small-to-moderate improvements in back-related function ($\text{SMD} = -0.44\text{ at }3\text{ months}$) and pain ($\text{MD} = -0.45$) compared to non-exercise controls; clinically equivalent to standard physical therapy exercise.
  • Patti et al. (2015) — Pilates for Chronic LBP:
    • Pilates exercise programs deliver small-to-moderate reductions in pain and disability; effective, but not superior to other active exercise forms.
  • Crawford et al. (2014) Pain Med — Active Self-Care CAM Therapies:
    • Evaluated mind-body CAM therapies (yoga, tai chi, meditation/mindfulness). Emphasizes that CAM therapies are most effective when they empower active patient engagement and self-efficacy rather than passive reliance on external providers.

6. Work & Occupational Health (Curriculum 3.8)

A. Interdisciplinary Rehabilitation & Return to Work (RTW)

  • Kamper et al. (2015) Cochrane / BMJ Review:
    • Intensive multidisciplinary biopsychosocial rehabilitation (physical conditioning + psychological CBT + workplace interventions) is superior to physical therapy alone or usual care.
    • Significantly increases the odds of returning to work at long-term follow-up ($>12\text{ months}$) with an Odds Ratio $\mathbf{\text{OR} = 1.87}$ (95% CI 1.39–2.53).
  • Gatchel et al. (2014) — Multidisciplinary vs. Interdisciplinary Teams:
    • Multidisciplinary: Clinicians from multiple disciplines work in parallel silos, with independent assessments and separate goals.
    • Interdisciplinary: Clinicians work as an integrated, collaborative team holding regular team conferences, resolving conflicting clinical messages, and sharing synchronized functional goals with the patient. Interdisciplinary care delivers superior outcomes and cost savings.

B. Physical Conditioning & Work Disability

  • Schaafsma et al. (2013) Cochrane Review — Physical Conditioning Programs for Workers with Back Pain:
    • Work-focused physical conditioning programs reduce sick leave by an average of 45 fewer days at 12-month follow-up compared to usual care in workers on subacute/chronic sick leave.
    • Inclusion of actual workplace visits and ergonomic job modifications dramatically enhances RTW success.
  • Oesch et al. (2010) Systematic Review: High-intensity conditioning programs incorporating functional job simulation reduce work disability in non-acute non-specific LBP.
