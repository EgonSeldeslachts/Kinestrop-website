Section 3 & 4: Treatment Evidence & Effect Size Master Table — EFIC Study Bible

This document is the granular clinical evidence and effect-size synthesis for the EFIC European Diploma in Pain Physiotherapy examination.

To answer EFIC multiple-choice questions correctly, you must memorize: 1. The exact GRADE / Cochrane evidence quality rating (e.g., Moderate-quality, Low-to-high, Inconclusive, No evidence). 2. The exact outcome-specific effect sizes (Differentiating Pain Intensity from Pain Interference, Disability, Depression, Anxiety, and Work / RTW). 3. The durability of effect (Short-term post-treatment vs. Long-term $\ge 12\text{ months}$). 4. The "⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula" provided for each study.

  1. De Kam (High / Moderate-to-High) = Autonomie, Gedrag & Synergie:
  2. Vuistregel: Hoe actiever de patiënt wordt en hoe breder het biopsychosociale kader (educatie + beweging + werk + angstextinctie), hoe hoger de evidentie en effectgrootte.

  3. Kernstudies: Multidisciplinair (Kamper), Aerobe training (Bidonde), PNE + oefenen (Moseley), CBT (Henschke), Exposure (Macedo).

  4. De Vallei (Low to Moderate) = Het Gelijkspel ("A vs B"):

  5. Vuistregel: Vergelijkt een studie twee actieve oefenvormen onderling (bv. Motor control vs Krachttraining, Yoga vs Wandelen) óf manuele therapie met oefentherapie? Het antwoord is ALTIJD Low-to-Moderate en klinisch gelijkwaardig. Er bestaat geen superieure 'wonderspier' of unieke techniek.

  7. Het Zinkgat (Very Low / Inconclusive / Rood) = De Passieve Stekker:

1. Education, Communication & Expectation

1.1 Detailed Evidence Matrix

📄 Petersen et al. (2014) — Meta-Analysis of Nocebo Hyperalgesia

  • Citation: Pain, 155(8):1426–34.
  • Evidence Quality: High internal validity (experimental randomized laboratory models).
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Overall Nocebo Hyperalgesia: Moderate-to-large effect.
  • Conditioning + Negative Verbal Suggestion: Moderate-to-large effect.
  • Negative Verbal Suggestion Alone: Moderate effect.
  • Conditioning Alone: Small-to-moderate effect.
  • Neurobiological Mechanism: Mediated by Cholecystokinin (CCK) and HPA axis activation; completely blocked or reversed by CCK antagonists (proglumide).
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: High internal validity | Moderate-to-large effect on pain sensitivity | Verbal + conditioning > Verbal alone | CCK-mediated, blocked by proglumide.

📄 Moseley & Butler (2015) — 15 Years of Explaining Pain

  • Citation: J Pain, 16(9):807–13.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate-to-high quality across multiple clinical trials.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Pain Biology Knowledge: Large effect.
  • Pain Catastrophizing (PCS) & Kinesiophobia (TSK): Moderate-to-large reduction.
  • Pain Intensity (Standalone PNE): Small reduction.
  • Pain Intensity (PNE + Active Movement): Moderate reduction.
  • Disability / Function (Standalone PNE): Small / Non-significant effect.
  • Disability / Function (PNE + Active Movement): Moderate reduction.
  • Healthcare Utilization: Moderate reduction in doctor consultations and diagnostic imaging requests.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Moderate-to-high quality | Large effect on knowledge | Moderate-to-large reduction in catastrophizing & kinesiophobia | Small effect on pain/disability as standalone → Moderate effect when combined with movement.

📄 Geneen et al. (2015) — Systematic Review of Pain Education in Chronic Pain

  • Citation: Syst Rev, 4:132.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate quality.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Pain Biology Knowledge: Large improvement.
  • Pain Intensity (Short-term $\le 3\text{m}$): Small reduction.
  • Physical Function / Disability: Small / Non-significant standalone effect.
  • Self-Efficacy: Small-to-moderate improvement.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Moderate quality | Large effect on knowledge | Small effect on short-term pain | No significant standalone effect on disability.

📄 Engers et al. (2008) — Cochrane Review of Patient Education for Low Back Pain

  • Citation: Cochrane Database Syst Rev, (1):CD003005.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate-to-high quality.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Acute Low Back Pain:
  • Brief patient education (e.g., The Back Book, 2-page advice sheet to stay active): Moderate effect on reducing sick leave and healthcare consultations; Small effect on pain.
  • Chronic Low Back Pain:
  • Brief generic advice/booklets: NO effect / Ineffective for pain or disability.
  • Intensive 1-on-1 or group biopsychosocial education: Moderate effect on functional disability and return to work.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Moderate-to-high quality | Acute LBP: Brief advice/booklet effective for sick leave | Chronic LBP: Brief advice INEFFECTIVE; intensive 1-on-1 biopsychosocial education required.

2. Behavioural, Psychological & Cognitive Interventions

2.1 Detailed Evidence Matrix

📄 Veehof et al. (2016) — Meta-Analysis of Acceptance & Mindfulness-Based Interventions

  • Citation: Cogn Behav Ther, 45(1):5–31.
  • Evidence Quality: Low-to-high quality (heterogeneous; 6 scored high, 5 scored low, none met all criteria; mean quality did not moderate effect).
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Pain Interference:
  • Post-treatment: Moderate effect.
  • Follow-up (2–6 months): Large effect.
  • Anxiety: Moderate effect.
  • Pain Intensity: Small effect.
  • Depression: Small effect.
  • Disability: Small effect.
  • Quality of Life: Small effect.
  • Subgroup Findings: ACT demonstrated significantly higher effect sizes on depression and anxiety than MBSR or MBCT.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Low-to-high quality evidence | Moderate effects on PAIN INTERFERENCE and ANXIETY | Small effects on pain intensity, depression, and disability | Pain interference effect grows to Large at follow-up.

📄 Macedo et al. (2010) — Graded Activity vs. Graded Exposure in Chronic LBP

  • Citation: Phys Ther, 90(6):860–79.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate quality.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Pain Intensity & Disability: Both Graded Activity (GA) and Graded Exposure (GExp) produce small-to-moderate improvements over minimal care.
  • Catastrophizing & Fear of Movement (TSK): Moderate-to-large reduction favoring Graded Exposure over Graded Activity in patients with high baseline fear.
  • Long-term Follow-up (12 months): Pain and disability outcomes between GA and GExp are equivalent.
  • Mechanisms:
  • Graded Activity: Operant conditioning using quota-based, time-contingent progression regardless of pain.
  • Graded Exposure: Cognitive-behavioral in-vivo confrontation based on fear hierarchies (e.g., PHODA) to violate threat expectations.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Moderate quality | GA and GExp equivalent for pain & disability at 12m | GExp superior for reducing catastrophizing & fear-avoidance in high-fear patients | GA = Operant quota; GExp = In-vivo fear hierarchy (PHODA).

📄 Henschke et al. (2010) — Cochrane Review of Behavioural Treatment for Chronic LBP

  • Citation: Cochrane Database Syst Rev, (7):CD002014.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate quality.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Pain Relief (vs. Waitlist): Moderate short-term effect.
  • Functional Improvement / Disability (vs. Waitlist): Moderate short-term effect.
  • Pain & Function (vs. Active PT / Usual Care): Small / Negligible difference when delivered in isolation.
  • Behavioral Therapy + Active Physical Exercise: Moderate long-term reduction in pain and disability.
  • Operant vs. Cognitive vs. Respondent: No significant difference between behavioral sub-types.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Moderate quality | Moderate short-term effect vs waitlist | Behavioral + Exercise superior to PT alone long-term | No difference between operant, cognitive, or respondent types.

📄 Eccleston et al. (2014) — Cochrane Review of Internet-Delivered Psychological Therapies

  • Citation: Cochrane Database Syst Rev, (2):CD010152.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate quality.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Pain Intensity (Post-treatment): Small reduction.
  • Disability / Function (Post-treatment): Moderate reduction.
  • Depression: Small-to-moderate reduction.
  • Anxiety: Small-to-moderate reduction.
  • Delivery Format: Therapist-supported internet CBT yielded significantly higher completion rates ($>75\%$) and larger effect sizes than unguided automated self-help.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Moderate quality | Moderate effect on disability | Small effect on pain and mood | Therapist-supported >> Unguided automated modules.

📄 Persson et al. (2008) — Relaxation for Chronic Musculoskeletal Pain

  • Citation: Phys Ther Rev, 13(5):355–365.
  • Evidence Quality: Low-to-moderate quality.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Pain Intensity: Small short-term reduction.
  • Muscle Guarding / Autonomic Arousal: Moderate short-term reduction.
  • Long-term Functional Recovery / Sick Leave: No significant effect / Inconclusive.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Low-to-moderate quality | Small short-term pain relief | Moderate reduction in somatic/muscle tension | Ineffective as standalone for long-term disability or work resumption.

📄 O'Keeffe et al. (2016) — Physical vs. Behavioral vs. Combined in Spinal Pain

  • Citation: J Pain, 17(7):755–74.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate quality.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Combined Interventions (Physical + Behavioral / Psychologically Informed):
  • Disability: Moderate effect.
  • Pain: Moderate effect.
  • Isolated Physical Interventions: Small-to-moderate short-term effects; lower 12-month durability.
  • Isolated Behavioral Interventions: Small-to-moderate on disability, minimal effect on physical capacity.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Moderate quality | Combined (Physical + Psychologically informed) superior to either alone | Highest long-term durability.

📄 Nicholas et al. (2011) & Linton & Shaw (2011) — The Flags Hierarchy

  • Citation: Phys Ther, 91(5):737–53 & 91(5):700–11.
  • Evidence Quality: Landmark epidemiological consensus framework.
  • The Flags Hierarchy for MCQ:
  • Red Flags: Serious biomedical pathology (infection, fracture, malignancy, cauda equina).
  • Orange Flags: Psychiatric illness (clinical depression, psychosis, personality disorders).
  • Yellow Flags: Psychological & cognitive beliefs (fear-avoidance, catastrophizing, low self-efficacy, expectation of harm).
  • Blue Flags: Workplace perceptions & social factors (unsupportive boss, high job demand, low control).
  • Black Flags: Systemic, financial, and legal barriers (workers' comp litigation, insurance delays, rigid sickness certification).
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Red = Pathology | Orange = Psychiatric | Yellow = Cognitive beliefs & fear | Blue = Workplace perceptions | Black = Systemic/Legal/Insurance.

3. Exercise & Physical Activity Prescription

3.1 Detailed Evidence Matrix

📄 Geneen et al. (2017) — Cochrane Overview of Exercise in Chronic Pain

  • Citation: Cochrane Database Syst Rev, 4:CD011279.
  • Evidence Quality: Low-to-moderate quality across diverse reviews.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Pain Severity: Small-to-moderate reduction.
  • Physical Function: Small-to-moderate improvement.
  • Quality of Life (HRQoL): Small improvement.
  • Adverse Events: Predominantly minor and transient (muscle soreness). Exercise is safe and first-line.
  • Modality Superiority: No single exercise type (aerobic vs. resistance vs. motor control) is consistently superior across all chronic pain syndromes.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Low-to-moderate quality overview | Small-to-moderate reduction in pain and disability across all pain conditions | Highly safe | No single exercise type universally superior.

📄 Bidonde et al. (2017) — Cochrane Review of Aerobic Exercise in Fibromyalgia

  • Citation: Cochrane Database Syst Rev, 6:CD009747.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate-quality evidence.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQoL on 0–100 FIQ): Moderate clinically meaningful improvement.
  • Pain Intensity (0–100 VAS): Small-to-moderate reduction.
  • Physical Function (0–100 scale): Moderate improvement ($\text{MD} = -6.4\text{ points}$).
  • Fatigue & Stiffness: Small / Borderline improvement.
  • Prescription Rule: Low-to-moderate intensity (walking, cycling, aquatic), progressing slowly to avoid post-exertional flare-ups.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: MODERATE-QUALITY evidence | Moderate improvement in HRQoL (MD -5.6 on FIQ) and function (MD -6.4) | Small-to-moderate pain reduction (MD -8.8) | Low-to-moderate intensity with gradual progression.

📄 Searle et al. (2015) — Exercise Modalities in Chronic Low Back Pain

  • Citation: Clin Rehabil, 29(12):1155–67.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate quality.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Pain Intensity:
  • Motor Control Exercise: Moderate reduction.
  • Resistance / Strengthening: Moderate reduction.
  • Aerobic Exercise: Small reduction.
  • Stretching / Flexibility: Small reduction.
  • Disability / Function:
  • Motor Control Exercise: Moderate reduction.
  • Resistance / Strengthening: Small-to-moderate reduction.
  • Long-term Equivalence: Differences diminish at 12 months; general physical activity yields comparable long-term functional recovery.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Moderate quality | Motor control & Resistance have slightly higher short-term effect sizes than aerobic | Equivalence achieved at 12 months.

📄 Oliveira et al. (2016) — Physical Activity vs. Exercise Therapy in MSK Pain

  • Citation: Arthritis Care Res, 68(12):1832–1842.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate quality.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Objectively Measured Physical Activity (Accelerometry):
  • Interventions specifically targeting daily physical activity: Moderate increase.
  • Routine supervised clinical exercise therapy: NO significant change in daily step count or habitual activity levels.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Moderate quality | Clinical exercise does NOT automatically increase daily physical activity | Interventions targeting daily habits increase steps by +1,210 steps/day | Accelerometry (4-7 days) required for objective tracking.

📄 Booth et al. (2017) — Biopsychosocial Exercise Prescription

  • Citation: Musculoskelet Care, 15(4):413–421.
  • Evidence Quality: Clinical framework & mechanistic synthesis.
  • Core Findings for MCQ:
  • Exercise-Induced Hypoalgesia (EIH): Healthy individuals experience reduced pain sensitivity post-exercise via endogenous opioid/endocannabinoid release. Patients with central sensitization often display dysfunctional EIH (hyperalgesia/flare-up post-exercise).
  • Clinical Strategy: De-emphasize biomechanical alignment and tissue damage; emphasize safe movement, graded exposure, and nervous system desensitization with very low initial thresholds.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Central sensitization causes dysfunctional EIH (post-exercise hyperalgesia) | Exercise acts via central neuro-desensitization, NOT tissue biomechanical fixing.

📄 Paper 15c: Wieland et al. (2017) — Yoga for Chronic Low Back Pain

Citation: Wieland LS, Skoetz N, Pilkington K, Vempati R, D'Adamo CR, Berman BM. Yoga treatment for chronic non-specific low back pain. Cochrane Database Syst Rev. 2017 Jan 12;1(1):CD010671. - Evidence Quality: Low-to-moderate quality across 12 RCTs. - Quantified Findings: - Back-specific function: Moderate improvement at 3 months. - Pain intensity: Small-to-moderate reduction ($ ext{MD} = -4.55/100 ext{ mm}$ at 3m; $-7.81/100$ at 6m). - Clinically equivalent to other active physical therapy exercise programs; superior to non-exercise educational control. - ⚡ MCQ Takeaway: Low-to-moderate evidence for small-to-moderate pain and functional improvements; equivalent to standard PT exercise.

4. Passive Modalities, Manual Therapy & Neuromodulation

4.1 Detailed Evidence Matrix

📄 Ebadi et al. (2014) — Cochrane Review of Therapeutic Ultrasound for Chronic LBP

  • Citation: Cochrane Database Syst Rev, (3):CD009169.
  • Evidence Quality: Low-to-moderate quality.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Pain Intensity vs. Placebo/Sham: No clinically meaningful difference.
  • Disability vs. Placebo/Sham: No statistically significant difference ($\text{MD} = 0.41$ on 0–24 Roland-Morris scale).
  • Clinical Verdict: NO supportive evidence; Therapeutic ultrasound is INEFFECTIVE and NOT recommended.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: 🚨 CLASSIC NEGATIVE TRAP: Low-to-moderate quality | NO evidence of benefit over sham (MD 2.16mm) | INEFFECTIVE and NOT recommended for chronic LBP.

📄 Rubinstein et al. (2011) — Cochrane Review of Spinal Manipulation (SMT) for Chronic LBP

  • Citation: Spine / Cochrane Database Syst Rev, 36(13):E825–46.
  • Evidence Quality: High-to-moderate quality.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Short-term Pain Relief (1 month): Small reduction.
  • Short-term Disability (1 month): Small reduction ($\text{MD} = -2.1\text{ points}$ on 0–100 scale).
  • Equivalence: SMT is equivalent to active exercise therapy, standard physiotherapy, and usual medical care. It is NOT superior.
  • Long-term Follow-up (12 months): No statistically significant difference in pain or disability compared to other active therapies.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: High-to-moderate quality | Small short-term effect on pain (MD -4.5mm) and disability (MD -2.1) | Equivalent to exercise and PT, NOT superior | No long-term advantage.

📄 Johnson et al. (2015) — Cochrane Review of TENS for Phantom Limb Pain

  • Citation: Cochrane Database Syst Rev, (8):CD007264.
  • Evidence Quality: Very low quality / INCONCLUSIVE.
  • Clinical Verdict: Formal evidence from randomized trials is inconclusive. TENS is justified as a pragmatic, low-risk empirical trial in individual patients, but definitive Cochrane confirmation of efficacy is lacking.
  • Neurobiology (from Sluka 2016):
  • High-frequency TENS ($>50\text{ Hz}$): Activates large $A\beta$ fibers $\rightarrow$ stimulates $\delta$-opioid receptors in spinal dorsal horn and RVM.
  • Low-frequency TENS ($<10\text{ Hz}$ / acupuncture-like): Stimulates motor and $A\delta$ fibers $\rightarrow$ stimulates $\mu$-opioid receptors in spinal cord and brainstem.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Very low / INCONCLUSIVE Cochrane evidence in phantom limb pain | Neurobiology: High-freq TENS = δ-opioid receptors; Low-freq TENS = μ-opioid receptors.

📄 Bowering et al. (2013) — Systematic Review of Graded Motor Imagery (GMI)

  • Citation: J Pain, 14(1):3–13.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate quality.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Pain Intensity & Disability: Large effect size favoring sequential GMI over standard medical care or usual physiotherapy.
  • Sequential Order Rule: Benefits occur ONLY when the 3 stages are delivered sequentially:
  • Implicit Motor Imagery: Left/Right Laterality Recognition.
  • Explicit Motor Imagery: Imagined movements.
  • Mirror Therapy: Visual feedback of unaffected limb.
  • Sequence Disruption: Introducing mirror therapy without prior implicit motor training can provoke symptom flares and increase cortical disorganization.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Moderate quality | Large effect size for pain & disability in CRPS I and phantom pain | Efficacy strictly depends on sequence: (1) Laterality → (2) Motor Imagery → (3) Mirror Therapy.

📄 Paper 19b: Jensen, Day, & Miró (2014) — Neuromodulatory Treatments for Chronic Pain

Citation: Jensen MP, Day MA, Miró J. Neuromodulatory treatments for chronic pain: efficacy and mechanisms. Nat Rev Neurol. 2014 Mar;10(3):167-78. - Evidence Level: Moderate-quality mechanistic and clinical review (tDCS, rTMS, neurofeedback). - Quantified Takeaways: Non-invasive brain stimulation over primary motor cortex ($M1$) alters thalamocortical dysrhythmia, delivering modest transient pain reduction. - Synergy: Efficacy and durability are significantly augmented when paired with active motor retraining. - ⚡ MCQ Takeaway: $M1$ stimulation modulates thalamocortical networks; optimal when combined with active exercise.

5. Multidisciplinary & Occupational Rehabilitation

5.1 Detailed Evidence Matrix

📄 Kamper et al. (2015) — Cochrane / BMJ Review of Multidisciplinary Biopsychosocial Rehab

  • Citation: BMJ, 350:h444 / Cochrane Database Syst Rev, (9):CD000963.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate-to-high quality.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Pain Reduction (at $>12\text{ months}$ vs. Usual Care): Moderate effect.
  • Pain Reduction (at $>12\text{ months}$ vs. Physical Treatments Alone): Small-to-moderate effect.
  • Disability Reduction (at $>12\text{ months}$ vs. Physical Treatments Alone): Small-to-moderate effect.
  • Work Status / Return to Work (at $>12\text{ months}$ vs. Physical Treatments Alone): Significantly higher odds of working.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Moderate-to-high quality | Multidisciplinary rehab is superior to monodisciplinary PT specifically at LONG-TERM (>12 months) follow-up | Significantly increases return to work (OR 1.87).

📄 Schaafsma et al. (2013) — Cochrane Review of Physical Conditioning for Work Outcomes

  • Citation: Cochrane Database Syst Rev, (8):CD001822.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate quality.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Sick Leave Days at 12 Months: Significant reduction in subacute/chronic workers compared to usual care.
  • Essential Component: Physical conditioning is effective only when workplace visits, ergonomics, and cognitive-behavioral work simulation are incorporated.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Moderate quality | Reduces sick leave by average 45 days at 12 months in subacute/chronic LBP | Must include cognitive-behavioral work simulation and workplace integration.

📄 Oesch et al. (2010) — Exercise & Work Disability in Persistent LBP

  • Citation: J Rehabil Med, 42(3):193–205.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate quality.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Work Disability / Days Off Work: Small-to-moderate reduction. High-intensity functional work conditioning outperformed low-intensity general exercise for work retention.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Moderate quality | Work disability reduced | High-intensity work-simulated exercise > Low-intensity general exercise for RTW.

📄 Richards et al. (2013) — Physiotherapy Functional Restoration in Post-Acute LBP

  • Citation: Man Ther, 18(1):4–25.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate quality.
  • Core Findings for MCQ:
  • Targeting workers in the post-acute / subacute window (4–12 weeks of sick leave) with intensive functional restoration programs prevents long-term chronification and disability compensation dependence.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Moderate quality | Optimal intervention window: Subacute 4–12 weeks off work | Intensive functional restoration prevents long-term disability transition.

📄 Khan et al. (2008) — Cochrane Review of Multidisciplinary Rehab Post Joint Replacement

  • Citation: Cochrane Database Syst Rev, (2):CD006073.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate quality.
  • Outcome-by-Outcome Quantified Effect Sizes:
  • Short-term Functional Activity: Inpatient multidisciplinary rehab produces small-to-moderate temporary improvements in complex patients.
  • Long-term Functional Recovery (12 months): NO statistically significant difference compared to routine outpatient / home-based physiotherapy.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Moderate quality | Inpatient multidisciplinary rehab post-arthroplasty yields short-term gains, but EQUIVALENT to home-based PT at 12 months.

📄 Gatchel et al. (2014) — Interdisciplinary vs. Multidisciplinary Pain Management

  • Citation: Am Psychol, 69(2):119–30.

  • Multidisciplinary: Healthcare professionals from different disciplines evaluate the patient independently; treatment occurs in parallel silos with separate goals.
  • Interdisciplinary: Healthcare professionals operate as a single coordinated team with face-to-face team conferencing, shared treatment goals, and integrated biopsychosocial execution.
  • Outcomes: True interdisciplinary care yields superior clinical outcomes, lower medication use, fewer surgeries, and major cost savings.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Multidisciplinary = Parallel silos with separate goals | Interdisciplinary = Integrated team with shared goals and team conferencing (superior outcomes & cost savings).

📄 Karp et al. (2008) & Herr (2011) — Pain Assessment & Pharmacology in Older Adults

  • Citation: Br J Anaesth, 101(1):111–20 & J Pain, 12(3 Suppl 1):S3–S13.
  • Evidence Quality: Geriatric pain management consensus.
  • Core Clinical Rules for MCQ:
  • Pharmacokinetics / Pharmacodynamics: Decreased renal clearance (GFR) and hepatic CYP450 metabolism $\rightarrow$ heightened drug accumulation and toxicity.
  • NSAIDs: Severe risks of GI ulceration/bleeding, acute renal failure, worsening hypertension, and congestive heart failure.
  • Opioids & TCAs: Severe risks of sedation, cognitive impairment, delirium, and falls. TCAs cause cardiac conduction delays (QT prolongation) and strong anticholinergic side effects.
  • Assessment Hierarchy: (1) Patient self-report (VRS/NRS) $\rightarrow$ (2) Underlying pathology/known painful conditions $\rightarrow$ (3) Observable pain behaviors $\rightarrow$ (4) Caregiver proxy reporting $\rightarrow$ (5) Empirical analgesic trial.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Non-pharmacological therapies preferred in elderly | NSAIDs = GI/Renal/Cardio toxicity | TCAs = QT prolongation & anticholinergic falls risk | Assessment hierarchy starts with self-report.

📄 Lichtner et al. (2014) — Pain Assessment for People with Dementia: Meta-Review of Tools

  • Citation: BMC Geriatr, 14:138. DOI: 10.1186/1471-2318-14-138.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate quality meta-review.
  • Key Evaluated Tools:
  • PAINAD (Pain Assessment in Advanced Dementia): 5 items (Breathing, Negative vocalization, Facial expression, Body language, Consolability); rapid (2–5 min), high inter-rater reliability, widely used in clinical nursing.
  • MOBID-2 (Mobility-Observed Information Dementia Pain Scale): Evaluates pain during active and guided movements across different body parts; highly sensitive to musculoskeletal pain.
  • PACSLAC (Pain Assessment Checklist for Seniors with Limited Ability to Communicate): Comprehensive checklist (60 items across 4 subscales); high validity, but more time-consuming.
  • Abbey Pain Scale & DOLOPLUS-2: Widely cited European observational scales; variable reporting of psychometric rigor.
  • Core Findings for MCQ:
  • No Single Gold Standard: Due to variability in study quality and lack of an objective reference standard in non-verbal patients, no single tool can be definitively recommended as superior over all others.
  • Behavioral vs. Physiological: Autonomic/physiological signs (HR, BP) are unreliable and non-specific; validated observational behavioral tools are mandatory.
  • Movement-Based Observation: Pain behaviors are significantly more apparent during guided movement/nursing care (as in MOBID-2) than at rest.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Meta-review of 28 tools | NO SINGLE TOOL can be recommended as the universal gold standard | Behavioral observation during movement (MOBID-2/PAINAD) >> Physiological signs.

📄 Hamill et al. (2014) — Pain-Location Tools (Body Maps) for Children

  • Citation: Pain, 155(8):1448–58. DOI: 10.1016/j.pain.2014.04.016.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate quality.
  • Core Findings for MCQ:
  • Age Threshold: Pain body maps and localization tools demonstrate robust validity, reliability, and clinical utility in children aged $\ge 4\text{ to }5\text{ years}$.
  • Below Age 4: Children lack the developmental spatial awareness and self-to-diagram body projection required to accurately map pain location.
  • Electronic vs. Paper: Electronic/tablet-based body maps demonstrate equal or higher completion rates, reliability, and child acceptability compared to paper drawings.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Moderate quality | Pediatric body maps are valid & reliable from age 4–5 years and up | Under age 4 cannot reliably map pain | Electronic maps equal or superior to paper.

📄 Hatfield & Ely (2015) — Measurement of Acute Pain in Infants

  • Citation: J Pediatr Nurs, 30(2):e71–80. DOI: 10.1016/j.pedn.2014.09.006.
  • Core Validated Infant Tools for MCQ:
  • FLACC (Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability): Validated in infants and non-verbal children aged 2 months to 7 years.
  • NIPS (Neonatal Infant Pain Scale): Evaluates facial expression, cry, breathing, arms, legs, state of arousal in preterm and term neonates.
  • PIPP (Premature Infant Pain Profile): Incorporates contextual gestational age and behavioral state with physiological (HR, $SpO_2$) and facial cues.
  • Core Findings for MCQ:
  • Behavioral responses (facial grimacing, crying, posturing) are more sensitive and specific than autonomic parameters alone.
  • Neurodevelopmental Impact: Inadequately treated repetitive acute pain in neonates produces persistent neuroarchitectural alterations in dorsal horn somatosensory processing and heightened pain sensitivity later in life.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: FLACC, NIPS, PIPP are validated infant tools | Behavioral cues > Autonomic signs alone | Untreated neonatal pain permanently alters nociceptive circuitry.

📄 Tyson & Brown (2013) — Measuring Pain in Neurological Conditions (Stroke, MS, SCI)

  • Citation: Clin Rehabil, 28(7):669–86. DOI: 10.1177/0269215513512217.
  • Evidence Quality: Moderate quality.
  • Core Findings for MCQ:
  • Psychometric Deficits: Few pain tools have been adequately tested for reliability and responsiveness specifically in central neurological conditions.
  • Hemispheric Stroke Tool Selection Rule:
  • Right-hemisphere lesions (visuospatial deficits, unilateral neglect, body schema distortion): Patients perform poorly on visual scales; prefer verbal rating scales (VRS) or written descriptors.
  • Left-hemisphere lesions (aphasia, verbal communication impairment): Patients cannot use verbal/written scales; prefer numeric (NRS), visual (VAS), or pictorial/faces scales.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Neurological pain tools lack robust psychometric testing | Right brain lesion / neglect = Use VERBAL scales (avoid visual) | Left brain lesion / aphasia = Use VISUAL/NUMERIC scales (avoid verbal).

📄 Gordon (2015) — Acute Pain Assessment: Moving Beyond Simple Pain Ratings

  • Citation: Curr Opin Anaesthesiol, 28(5):565–9. DOI: 10.1097/ACO.0000000000000225.
  • Evidence Quality: Clinical practice and health policy review.
  • Core Findings for MCQ:
  • Treating pain strictly as the "fifth vital sign" using solitary 0–10 Numeric Rating Scales led to over-reliance on opioid prescribing and failed to evaluate physical recovery.
  • Acute pain assessment must evaluate functional interference (e.g., ability to deep breathe, cough, ambulate, and sleep) rather than pursuing a zero pain score at rest.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Solitary 0–10 numeric scores at rest are insufficient | Must assess pain on movement and functional capacity (coughing, ambulation, ADLs).

📄 Verbunt et al. (2012) — Physical Activity Assessment by Movement Registration Systems

  • Citation: Clin J Pain, 28(7):643–54. DOI: 10.1097/AJP.0b013e31823bb35c.
  • Core Findings for MCQ:
  • Subjective vs. Objective Discordance: Self-report diaries and questionnaires correlate poorly with objective accelerometry; patients with high pain catastrophizing and fear-avoidance systematically overestimate or underestimate their true physical activity.
  • Monitoring Duration: A minimum of 4 to 7 consecutive days (including weekdays and weekends) of triaxial accelerometry is required to achieve reliable, representative physical activity profiling.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Self-reports correlate poorly with objective activity | Valid accelerometry requires 4 to 7 consecutive days of continuous measurement.

📄 Smart et al. (2011) — Clinical Discriminative Validity: Mechanisms-Based Pain Classification

  • Citation: Man Ther, 16(4):302–8 & 17(4):336–44.
  • Evidence Quality: High-to-moderate clinical validity.
  • The Three Mechanism Clusters for MCQ:
  • Nociceptive Pain: Clear anatomical proportionality between pain and mechanical stress; intermittent/sharp with movement, dull ache at rest; absence of dysesthesias; identifiable aggravating/easing mechanical factors.
  • Peripheral Neuropathic Pain: Pain in dermatomal or cutaneous nerve distribution; history of nerve injury or compression; sensory abnormalities (allodynia, hyperalgesia, numbness) mapped to nerve territory; positive neural provocation tests (SLR, Upper Limb Neurodynamic Tests).
  • Central Sensitization Pain: Disproportionate, non-mechanical, unpredictable pain pattern; widespread neuroanatomical distribution (non-dermatomal); diffuse tenderness to light palpation; high association with maladaptive psychosocial factors (catastrophizing, distress) and central symptoms (fatigue, sleep disturbance, hyperreactivity to sensory stimuli).
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Nociceptive = Clear mechanical/anatomical proportionality | Peripheral Neuropathic = Dermatomal/nerve distribution + neurodynamic findings | Central Sensitization = Disproportionate, widespread, non-mechanical + hyperreactivity.

📄 Finnerup et al. (2016) — Neuropathic Pain: Updated Grading System for Clinical & Research Practice

  • Citation: Pain, 157(8):1599–606. DOI: 10.1097/j.pain.0000000000000600.
  • Evidence Quality: IASP NeuPSIG International Consensus Classification.
  • Diagnostic Grading Steps (Essential for MCQ):
  • Step 1: Pain with a distinct neuroanatomically plausible distribution + History suggestive of a relevant neurological lesion or disease.
  • Possible Neuropathic Pain: Satisfies Step 1 (History + Neuroanatomical distribution). No clinical examination yet required.
  • Probable Neuropathic Pain: Satisfies Step 1 + Demonstration of distinct sensory signs (positive or negative sensory signs like hypoesthesia, allodynia, pinprick hyperalgesia) confined to the neuroanatomical distribution of the lesion during clinical examination.
  • Definite Neuropathic Pain: Satisfies Step 1 + Step 2 + Objective confirmatory diagnostic test proving the lesion/disease (e.g., MRI showing nerve root compression, biopsy showing small fiber neuropathy, EMG/nerve conduction studies, surgical confirmation).
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Possible = Plausible history + Anatomical distribution | Probable = Adds clinical sensory examination signs | Definite = Adds objective diagnostic confirmation (EMG/MRI/Biopsy).

📄 Simons et al. (2014) & Malfliet et al. (2017) — Neural Systems of Psychological Processing & Brain Changes

  • Citations: Neurosci Biobehav Rev, 39:61–78 & Eur J Pain, 21(5):769–86.
  • Evidence Quality: Systematic reviews of functional neuroimaging and structural morphometry.
  • Key Neural Circuitry for MCQ:
  • Fear-Avoidance Brain Circuit: Pain-related fear and perceived threat activate the Amygdala and Dorsal Anterior Cingulate Cortex (dACC), disrupting normal prefrontal inhibitory control.
  • Reward Deficiency Syndrome: Chronic pain impairs dopaminergic signaling in the Nucleus Accumbens (ventral striatum) and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (vmPFC), leading to anhedonia and loss of reward-driven active behavior.
  • Catastrophizing & Structural Morphometry (Malfliet 2017): High pain catastrophizing is directly associated with reduced gray matter volume in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) (impairing top-down pain gating) and hyperactivity in the default mode network (DMN) during rest.
  • ⚡ MCQ Memorization Formula: Threat/Fear = Amygdala + dACC hyperactivity | Chronic inactivity = Nucleus Accumbens reward signaling deficiency | High Catastrophizing = DLPFC gray matter thinning + impaired top-down descending inhibition.

10. Top 20 EFIC MCQ Distractor Traps & Rules

  1. The Acceptance & Mindfulness Trap (Veehof 2016):
  2. Trap: "Acceptance- and mindfulness-based interventions demonstrate high-quality evidence for moderate reductions in pain intensity."
  3. Reality: FALSE. Evidence quality is low-to-high. Effect on pain intensity is small. Moderate effects occur ONLY on pain interference and anxiety.
  4. The Ultrasound Trap (Ebadi 2014):
  5. Trap: "Cochrane reviews recommend therapeutic ultrasound as an effective adjunctive treatment for chronic low back pain."
  6. Reality: FALSE. Cochrane review found NO evidence of benefit over sham. Ultrasound is ineffective and not recommended.
  7. The SMT Superiority Trap (Rubinstein 2011):
  8. Trap: "Spinal manipulative therapy (SMT) is clinically superior to exercise therapy for chronic low back pain."
  9. Reality: FALSE. SMT produces small short-term improvements that are equivalent to exercise and standard medical care, NOT superior.
  10. The GMI Sequence Trap (Bowering 2013):
  11. Trap: "In severe CRPS, mirror therapy should be instituted immediately to provide rapid visual cortical feedback."
  12. Reality: FALSE. GMI must follow the strict sequential order: (1) Laterality recognition $\rightarrow$ (2) Imagined movements $\rightarrow$ (3) Mirror therapy. Disrupting the sequence worsens symptoms.
  13. The Multidisciplinary Durability Trap (Kamper 2015):
  14. Trap: "Multidisciplinary biopsychosocial rehabilitation shows its greatest advantage over physical therapy in the first 2 to 4 weeks of treatment."
  15. Reality: FALSE. Multidisciplinary rehab shows its primary advantage in long-term follow-up ($>12\text{ months}$), specifically for sustained return to work ($\text{OR} = 1.87$) and reduced sick leave.
  16. The Education Equivalence Trap (Engers 2008):
  17. Trap: "Brief educational booklets (like The Back Book) are equally effective for acute and chronic low back pain."
  18. Reality: FALSE. Brief advice is effective for acute LBP, but ineffective for chronic LBP (which requires intensive, structured biopsychosocial education).
  19. The Standalone PNE Trap (Moseley 2015, Geneen 2015):
  20. Trap: "Pain Neuroscience Education delivered as a standalone lecture is sufficient to eliminate chronic pain-related disability."
  21. Reality: FALSE. Standalone PNE improves knowledge and catastrophizing, but has minimal impact on disability unless paired with active movement and functional exposure.
  22. The Nocebo Mechanism Trap (Petersen 2014):
  23. Trap: "Nocebo hyperalgesia is purely a psychological construct with no identifiable neurochemical basis."
  24. Reality: FALSE. Nocebo hyperalgesia has a moderate-to-large effect size, is biochemically mediated by the Cholecystokinin (CCK) system, and is blocked by proglumide.
  25. The Exercise Superiority Trap (Searle 2015, Geneen 2017):
  26. Trap: "Specific core motor control exercise is proven to be substantially superior to all other exercise forms for long-term chronic back pain relief."
  27. Reality: FALSE. Motor control and resistance exercises show slightly higher short-term effects, but long-term outcomes (12 months) are broadly equivalent across active exercise modalities.
  28. The Graded Activity vs. Exposure Trap (Macedo 2010):
  29. Trap: "Graded Activity and Graded Exposure operate on the identical psychological mechanism."
  30. Reality: FALSE. Graded Activity uses operant conditioning (quota-based pacing). Graded Exposure uses cognitive-behavioral in-vivo threat disconfirmation (fear hierarchies / PHODA).
  31. The Pediatric Solicitousness Trap (Eccleston 2015):
  32. Trap: "Treating the child with chronic pain is sufficient; parental responses have no measurable impact on clinical outcomes."
  33. Reality: FALSE. Parental solicitousness directly maintains child disability; parent-targeted psychological interventions significantly reduce parental distress and improve child school attendance.
  34. The TENS in Phantom Limb Trap (Johnson 2015):
  35. Trap: "Cochrane reviews confirm high-quality proof that TENS completely resolves phantom limb pain."
  36. Reality: FALSE. Cochrane reviews classify RCT evidence for TENS in phantom limb pain as inconclusive.
  37. The NSAID in Neuropathic Pain Trap:
  38. Trap: "High-dose NSAIDs are recommended as first-line therapy for neuropathic pain and central sensitization."
  39. Reality: FALSE. NSAIDs target peripheral prostaglandins; they have no efficacy in neuropathic pain or central sensitization and carry severe gastrointestinal and renal risks.
  40. The Dementia Pain Measurement Trap (Lichtner 2014, Herr 2011):
  41. Trap: "In patients with severe Alzheimer's dementia, heart rate and blood pressure fluctuations are superior to behavioral observation tools."
  42. Reality: FALSE. Autonomic signs are non-specific and habituate; validated observational scales (PAINAD, MOBID-2, Abbey) are gold standard when self-report fails.
  43. The Inpatient Arthroplasty Rehab Trap (Khan 2008):
  44. Trap: "All post-total knee arthroplasty patients should undergo routine inpatient multidisciplinary rehabilitation for superior 1-year outcomes."
  45. Reality: FALSE. Routine inpatient multidisciplinary rehabilitation does not outperform home-based exercise at 12 months.
  46. The Daily Activity Assumption Trap (Oliveira 2016):
  47. Trap: "Patients who complete 3 physical therapy exercise sessions a week automatically increase their daily physical activity levels."
  48. Reality: FALSE. Standard exercise therapy does not change habitual daily physical activity unless daily behavioral pacing and tracking are explicitly prescribed.
  49. The Dementia Gold Standard Trap (Lichtner 2014):
  50. Trap: "The PAINAD scale has been confirmed as the definitive gold standard observational instrument across all dementia populations."
  51. Reality: FALSE. Meta-review of 28 tools demonstrates that NO single tool can be recommended as a universal gold standard due to widespread psychometric variability.
  52. The Post-Surgical Risk Factor Trap (Wang 2016):
  53. Trap: "Mastectomy carries a significantly higher risk of persistent post-surgical pain than breast-conserving lumpectomy, and older age is a risk factor."
  54. Reality: FALSE. Surgical type (mastectomy vs. lumpectomy) and chemotherapy have no significant association with persistent pain. Younger age increases risk ($\text{OR} = 1.36$ per decade younger), and axillary lymph node dissection (ALND) is the strongest predictor ($\text{OR} = 2.41$).
  55. The Neurological Scale Selection Trap (Tyson 2013):
  56. Trap: "Patients with right-hemisphere stroke lesions perform best on visual analog and pictorial scales."
  57. Reality: FALSE. Right-hemisphere stroke patients frequently suffer from spatial neglect and visual distortion; they perform better on verbal rating scales (VRS). Patients with left-hemisphere stroke (aphasia) perform better on visual or numeric scales.
  58. The Possible Neuropathic Pain Trap (Finnerup 2016):
  59. Trap: "Diagnosing 'Possible Neuropathic Pain' requires objective confirmatory tests such as EMG or nerve biopsy."

  60. Reality: FALSE. 'Possible' requires only a plausible history and neuroanatomically compatible distribution. Clinical sensory signs make it 'Probable'. Confirmatory diagnostic tests are required for 'Definite'.

  61. The Electronic Activity Feedback Trap (Oliveira 2018):

  62. Trap: "Providing chronic MSK pain patients with wearable activity trackers and smartphone feedback apps is an effective standalone intervention to reduce pain and disability."
  63. Reality: FALSE. Meta-analysis (Arch Phys Med Rehabil 2018) shows electronic feedback alone is ineffective in reducing pain or disability without clinician-led behavioral pacing and counseling.
  64. The Exercise in Central Sensitization Trap (Lima 2017, Sluka 2018):
  65. Trap: "Intense exercise always produces exercise-induced hypoalgesia regardless of the patient's pain mechanism."
  66. Reality: FALSE. In patients with central sensitization (fibromyalgia, CWP), unaccustomed or vigorous eccentric exercise causes exercise-induced hyperalgesia (flare-up) due to deficient descending inhibition (impaired CPM) and heightened dorsal horn excitability. Prescription must start with low-intensity aerobic or submaximal isometrics ($20\text{–}25\%$ MVC).
  67. The Cancer Pain Recurrence Trap (De Groef 2019):
  68. Trap: "Any persistent musculoskeletal or neuropathic pain after breast cancer treatment should be presumed to indicate cancer recurrence and exercise should be ceased."
  69. Reality: FALSE. Persistent pain after cancer (PPAC) is common (~30–50%) due to surgical trauma, radiation fibrosis, aromatase inhibitors, and CIPN. Supervised exercise is safe and effective; PNE must explicitly reassure patients that non-tumoral altered neurobiology explains persistent symptoms.
  70. The Pediatric Return-to-School Trap (Harrison 2019):
  71. Trap: "Children with chronic pain should remain absent from school until their pain score drops below 3/10."
  72. Reality: FALSE. Best-evidence pediatric rehabilitation is function-first; school attendance is prioritized regardless of pain intensity via structured graded return-to-school (RTS) plans. Waiting for pain resolution promotes severe social disability and isolation.
  73. The Community Dementia Agitation Trap (Bullock 2019):
  74. Trap: "Antipsychotic medications are first-line for new-onset agitation and behavioral disturbance in dementia patients."
  75. Reality: FALSE. Agitation is frequently an atypical manifestation of untreated physical pain. A structured stepwise pain management protocol (STA-OP, starting with scheduled paracetamol) significantly reduces behavioral agitation without antipsychotic risks.
  76. The Contextual Factors Trap (Testa & Rossettini 2016):
  77. Trap: "Placebo effects in physiotherapy are illusory noise that clinicians should ignore or attempt to eliminate."
  78. Reality: FALSE. Contextual factors trigger real, measurable neurobiological analgesia ($g \approx 0.5\text{–}0.7$) mediated by endogenous opioids and endocannabinoids. Clinicians have an ethical responsibility to maximize positive contextual cues while avoiding deceit.
