Section 5: Interprofessional Working and Learning — EFIC Ingest & Study Guide

This module covers team care models, collaborative clinical communication, shared decision making, and reflective practice required for Section Five: Interprofessional Working and Learning of the official EFIC Core Curriculum.

1. Healthcare Team Models & Collaborative Care (Curriculum 5.1)

A. Team Typologies: Unidisciplinary, Multidisciplinary, Interdisciplinary, and Transdisciplinary

The EFIC examination rigorously tests the distinctions between models of healthcare delivery:

Model Organizational Structure Communication & Goal Setting Clinical Outcomes & Cost-Effectiveness
Unidisciplinary Single healthcare professional working in isolation (e.g., solo private physical therapy practice). No interprofessional communication. Limited scope; high risk of treatment failure in complex chronic pain.
Multidisciplinary Multiple healthcare professionals from different disciplines assess and treat the patient independently (silos). Parallel / Fragmented: Clinicians write independent notes; separate disciplinary goals; rare direct interaction. High risk of conflicting clinical messages to the patient. Modest improvements; moderate risk of patient confusion and over-treatment.
Interdisciplinary Integrated, collaborative team (physician, physiotherapist, clinical psychologist, occupational therapist, nurse) working together. Synchronized / Collaborative: Formal team case conferences; shared, unified biopsychosocial goals; coordinated patient messaging; resolved conflicts. Gold Standard (Kamper 2015, Gatchel 2014): Superior long-term outcomes, highest RTW rates ($\text{OR}=1.87$), greatest reduction in healthcare utilization.
Transdisciplinary Team members share and blend traditional disciplinary boundaries, training one another in core skills. Role Release / Blended: A primary key worker delivers multi-faceted care with active cross-disciplinary coaching. Highly efficient in community, palliative, and pediatric community settings.

📄 Paper 35: Gatchel et al. (2014) — Interdisciplinary Chronic Pain Management

Citation: Gatchel RJ, McGeary DD, McGeary CA, Lippe B. Interdisciplinary chronic pain management: past, present, and future. Am Psychol. 2014;69(2):119-30.

  • Core Distinctions:
  • Multidisciplinary programs often fail to produce durable behavioral change because each clinician operates from their own biomedical or disciplinary silo, leaving the patient to reconcile contradictory advice.
  • Interdisciplinary pain rehabilitation programs (IPRPs) conduct regular face-to-face team meetings where conflicting messages (e.g., medical advice to rest vs. physical therapy advice to move) are resolved before communicating with the patient.
  • Delivers significant reductions in opioid dependency, dramatic decreases in spinal surgeries, and durable return to work.

2. Interprofessional Communication & Clinical Handover (Curriculum 5.1)

A. Structured Communication: The SBAR Framework

In high-stakes pain management and interprofessional clinical referrals, unstructured communication leads to medical errors, missed red flags, and delayed care. The SBAR tool is the internationally recognized standard:

  • S — Situation:
  • Concise statement of the immediate problem: who you are, the patient's name, and the primary clinical reason for contact ("I am calling regarding patient X with chronic LBP who has developed acute new-onset saddle hypoesthesia today").
  • B — Background:
  • Relevant clinical context: date of onset, prior surgeries, relevant medical diagnoses, current medications, baseline functional status.
  • A — Assessment:
  • Objective clinical findings: physical examination signs, neurological deficits, sensory changes, vital signs, red/orange flag indicators ("Clinical exam shows bilateral absent Achilles reflexes, 4/10 motor power in ankle dorsiflexion, and decreased perineal pinprick sensation").
  • R — Recommendation:
  • Actionable proposal: specific request, urgency, timeframe ("I recommend an urgent MRI of the lumbosacral spine and emergency spinal surgical consultation today for suspected cauda equina compression").

B. Resolving Conflicting Health Advice & Protecting the Therapeutic Alliance

  • A primary iatrogenic cause of pain chronification is conflicting clinical information (e.g., radiologist reports severe degenerative disc disease, surgeon recommends spinal fusion, and physical therapist encourages active bending).
  • Clinical Best Practice:
  • Acknowledge and Validate: Validate the patient's confusion without attacking the other clinician's integrity ("I can understand why hearing different things has made you feel anxious").
  • Recontextualize Findings: Frame imaging findings as normal age-related changes (like grey hair on the inside) that do not dictate physical capacity.
  • Proactive Direct Interprofessional Contact: Directly contact the referring physician or surgeon to align on consistent, reassuring rehabilitation goals.

3. Shared Decision Making (SDM) in Pain Management (Curriculum 5.1)

A. The 3-Step Clinical Model of Shared Decision Making (Elwyn et al. 2012)

Shared decision making is an ethical and clinical necessity in preference-sensitive chronic pain care: 1. Step 1: Choice Talk (Establishing that a choice exists): - The clinician explicitly informs the patient that multiple legitimate management options exist, and that their personal values and preferences are essential in guiding the decision ("There is no single 'magic fix' for chronic back pain; there are several paths we can take, and your goals will determine the best choice"). 2. Step 2: Option Talk (Comparing alternatives): - The clinician provides balanced, evidence-based information regarding the risks, benefits, uncertainties, and commitments of each option (e.g., active exercise therapy + PNE vs. spinal injection vs. surgical consultation). - Use of standardized Patient Decision Aids (PDAs) to quantify absolute risks and benefits. 3. Step 3: Decision Talk (Reaching an agreed path): - Eliciting what matters most to the patient (returning to work, playing with children, reducing medication side effects) and supporting them to choose and commit to a personalized treatment plan.

4. Reflective Practice, Supervision & Continuing Professional Development (Curriculum 5.1)

A. Reflective Practice Frameworks (Gibbs' Reflective Cycle)

Effective pain physiotherapists systematically analyze complex clinical encounters to mitigate burnout, address countertransference, and refine clinical reasoning: 1. Description: What happened during the clinical encounter? 2. Feelings: What were you thinking and feeling (frustration, helplessness, anxiety)? 3. Evaluation: What was good and bad about the experience? 4. Analysis: What sense can you make of the situation using pain science and behavioral theory? 5. Conclusion: What else could you have done? 6. Action Plan: If it arose again, what specific strategy would you employ?

B. Clinical Mentoring & Countertransference in Chronic Pain

  • Countertransference: Emotional reactions provoked in the clinician by the patient's distress, high catastrophizing, or demanding behaviors. In chronic pain, clinicians frequently experience feelings of inadequacy, frustration, or therapeutic nihilism.
  • Regular Interprofessional Supervision: Vital for maintaining empathy, avoiding cynical detachment, preventing clinician burnout, and ensuring objective, evidence-based practice.
