Section 4: Populations in Pain & Specific Conditions — EFIC Ingest & Study Guide

This module covers the epidemiology, evidence-based management, and specialized assessment protocols for Section Four: Populations in Pain of the official EFIC Core Curriculum.

1. Epidemiology & Specific Clinical Pain Conditions (Curriculum 4.1 & 4.2)

A. Epidemiology of Chronic Pain in Europe (Curriculum 4.1)

  • Prevalence: Chronic pain affects approximately $19\%\text{–}20\%$ of the European adult population (Breivik et al. 2006).
  • Demographic Predictors: Higher prevalence in females, advancing age, lower socioeconomic status, and manual labor occupations.
  • Societal Impact: One of the leading causes of years lived with disability (YLD) globally; direct medical costs and indirect productivity losses account for $>1.5\text{–}3\%$ of European GDP.

B. Fibromyalgia & Chronic Widespread Pain (Curriculum 4.2.1)

  • ACR 2010 / 2016 Diagnostic Criteria:
    • Diagnosed without requiring manual tender point examination.
    • Criteria met if:
      1. Widespread Pain Index (WPI) $\ge 7$ and Symptom Severity Scale (SSS) $\ge 5$, OR WPI 3–6 and SSS $\ge 9$.
      2. Generalized pain defined as pain in at least 4 out of 5 regions (left upper, right upper, left lower, right lower, and axial).
      3. Symptoms present at a similar level for at least 3 months.
      4. Diagnosis of fibromyalgia is valid irrespective of other diagnoses (does not exclude other clinical illnesses).
  • Core Pathophysiology: Prototype of nociplastic pain — characterized by reduced central descending inhibition (deficient CPM), heightened temporal summation of pain (TSP), elevated substance P and glutamate in CSF, and functional connectivity changes in the default mode network.
  • Evidence-Based Rehabilitation (EULAR Recommendations & Bidonde 2017):
    • First-line therapy: Graded aerobic exercise (moderate-quality Cochrane evidence; Bidonde 2017) and strength training.
    • Multicomponent therapy: Aerobic exercise + Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT) + Pain Neuroscience Education (PNE).
    • Pharmacotherapy is secondary (duloxetine, milnacipran, pregabalin; opioids are strongly contraindicated).

C. Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS Types I & II) (Curriculum 4.2.2)

Papers: Smart KM, Wand BM, O'Connell NE. Cochrane Database Syst Rev, 2016; Méndez-Rebolledo G et al. J Back Musculoskelet Rehabil, 2017.

  • Diagnostic Distinction (Budapest Diagnostic Criteria):
    • CRPS Type I (Reflex Sympathetic Dystrophy): Develops after an initiating noxious event (fracture, sprain, surgery) or immobilization without verifiable major peripheral nerve injury.
    • CRPS Type II (Causalgia): Develops following identifiable major peripheral nerve injury (e.g., median or tibial nerve transection).
    • Budapest Diagnostic Categories (Must exhibit symptoms in $\ge 3$ categories and signs in $\ge 2$ categories):
      1. Sensory: Hyperalgesia (to pinprick) and/or allodynia (to light touch/temperature/deep somatic pressure).
      2. Vasomotor: Temperature asymmetry ($>1^\circ\text{C}$) and/or skin color changes / asymmetry.
      3. Sudomotor / Edema: Edema and/or sweating changes / sweating asymmetry.
      4. Motor / Trophic: Decreased range of motion and/or motor dysfunction (weakness, tremor, dystonia) and/or trophic changes (hair, nail, skin changes).
  • Evidence-Based Physiotherapy:
    • Graded Motor Imagery (GMI) & Mirror Therapy: Moderate-quality evidence for reducing pain and disability in CRPS 1. Must follow the strict sequence: (1) Laterality recognition $\rightarrow$ (2) Imagined movements $\rightarrow$ (3) Mirror therapy.
    • Tactile Sensory Discrimination: Touching varying textures or localization on skin restores somatotopic representation in $S1$.
    • Clinical Warning: Aggressive passive stretching and painful manual joint mobilization during acute inflammatory flare phases exacerbate neurogenic inflammation and are contraindicated.

2. Cancer Pain & Rehabilitation (Curriculum 4.2.2)

📄 Paper 28: De Groef et al. (2019) — Best-Evidence Rehabilitation for Cancer Pain

Citation: De Groef A, Penen F, Dams L, Van der Gucht E, Nijs J, Meeus M. Best-Evidence Rehabilitation for Chronic Pain Part 2: Pain during and after Cancer Treatment. J Clin Med. 2019 Jul 7;8(7):979.

  • Etiology of Pain in Cancer Survivors:
    • Pain during cancer: Tumor invasion, bone metastases (osteoclast activation), visceral obstruction.
    • Pain post-cancer treatment (Persistent Pain After Cancer / PPAC):
      1. Surgery: Post-mastectomy pain syndrome (intercostobrachial neuralgia), thoracotomy pain, phantom breast pain.
      2. Chemotherapy: Chemotherapy-Induced Peripheral Neuropathy (CIPN) caused by taxanes, platinum compounds, and vinca alkaloids (symmetric "glove-and-stocking" sensory loss, tingling, burning, cold allodynia).
      3. Radiotherapy: Microvascular fibrosis, radiation-induced plexopathy, joint contractures.
      4. Hormone Therapy: Aromatase inhibitor-induced arthralgia (AIA) affecting up to $50\%$ of women treated for breast cancer.
  • Evidence-Based Physical Therapy Interventions:
    1. Supervised Exercise (Aerobic & Resistance):
      • Strong evidence that exercise during and post-chemotherapy/radiotherapy significantly reduces cancer-related fatigue, neuropathic discomfort, and pain while improving functional capacity and quality of life.
    2. Manual Therapy & Passive Mobilization:
      • Gentle myofascial release and passive mobilization improve shoulder range of motion and scar mobility post-mastectomy, but must respect tissue healing and lymphedema risk.
    3. Lymphedema Management:
      • Combined decongestive therapy (compression garments, manual lymphatic drainage, skin care, remedial exercise) reduces pain and limb heaviness.
    4. Cancer-Specific Pain Neuroscience Education (PNE):
      • Cancer survivors suffer severe fear of disease recurrence. PNE must specifically address the fear that persistent pain indicates cancer relapse, reassuring that altered nervous system sensitivity rather than tumor activity is generating the pain signals.

3. Older Adults & Dementia (Curriculum 4.3 & 4.4)

📄 Paper 29: Makris et al. (2014) — Persistent Pain in the Older Patient (JAMA)

Citation: Makris UE, Abrams RC, Gurland B, Reid MC. Management of persistent pain in the older patient: a clinical review. JAMA. 2014 Aug 27;312(8):825-36.

  • Age-Related Clinical Realities:
    • Older adults suffer high rates of multimorbidity (osteoarthritis, cardiovascular disease, renal insufficiency, diabetes, cognitive decline).
    • High risk of polypharmacy and severe drug-drug / drug-disease interactions.
  • Pharmacological Vulnerabilities (Essential for EFIC MCQs):
    • NSAIDs: High risk of life-threatening gastrointestinal hemorrhage, acute renal failure, fluid retention, and worsening of congestive heart failure. Systemic oral NSAIDs should be avoided or used at lowest dose for shortest duration; topical NSAIDs preferred for knee/hand OA.
    • Opioids: Increased risk of falls, hip fractures, cognitive delirium, constipation/impaction, and respiratory depression.
    • Tricyclic Antidepressants (TCAs - Amitriptyline): High anticholinergic burden (confusion, urinary retention, dry mouth, glaucoma exacerbation) and cardiac conduction prolongation (QT interval).
  • First-Line Recommended Strategy:
    • Non-pharmacological therapies are first-line: regular tailored physical activity, aquatic therapy, balance and resistance training, cognitive-behavioral coping, and home safety modifications.

📄 Paper 30: Bullock et al. (2019) — Pain in People with Dementia

Citation: Bullock L, Bedson J, Jordan JL, Bartlam B, Chew-Graham CA, Campbell P. Pain assessment and pain treatment for community-dwelling people with dementia: A systematic review and narrative synthesis. Int J Geriatr Psychiatry. 2019 Jun;34(6):807-821.

  • Core Challenges:
    • Pain is severely under-detected and undertreated in community-dwelling individuals with dementia.
    • Pain often manifests atypically as neuropsychiatric symptoms: agitation, aggression, sleep disruption, vocalization, wandering, or withdrawal.
  • Assessment & Treatment Principles:
    1. Family and Caregiver Collateral History: Caregivers are essential in detecting subtle changes from baseline behavior (e.g., refusing to eat, resisting dressing).
    2. Stepwise Treatment Protocol: Implementing a structured stepwise pain protocol (starting with regular paracetamol/acetaminophen before escalating) significantly reduces agitation and behavioral disturbances without requiring antipsychotics.
    3. Non-Pharmacological Strategies: Structured sensory interventions (music, gentle touch), pleasant scheduled activities, and regular guided movement reduce both pain and behavioral distress.

📄 Paper 31: Lichtner et al. (2014) & Herr (2011) — Dementia Assessment Tools & Hierarchy

  • Herr (2011) Geriatric Hierarchy of Pain Assessment Techniques:
    1. Self-Report: Always attempt first (use Verbal Rating Scale [VRS] or large-print horizontal NRS).
    2. Search for Pathology: Identify potential painful sources (arthritis, occult fracture, pressure sore, UTI, constipation).
    3. Observe Behaviors: Assess facial expressions (brow furrowing, orbital tightening, nose wrinkling), vocalizations, guarding.
    4. Surrogate Reporting: Consult family members or primary nursing staff.
    5. Empirical Analgesic Trial: Administer low-dose non-opioid analgesic and observe for functional/behavioral improvement.
  • Observational Dementia Pain Tools (Lichtner et al. 2014 Meta-Review):
    • No universal gold standard: Studies vary widely; no single tool is superior.
    • Movement-Based Evaluation: Pain behaviors must be observed during movement and functional care (transferring, walking) rather than solely at rest.
    • Validated Scales:
      • PAINAD: 5 items (Breathing, Vocalization, Facial expression, Body language, Consolability); quick 0–10 scoring.
      • MOBID-2: Mobilization-observed checklist across 10 body regions; excellent construct validity for musculoskeletal pain.
      • PACSLAC: 60-item comprehensive checklist; highly sensitive across diverse clinical settings.

4. Infants, Children, and Adolescents (Curriculum 4.3 & 4.5)

📄 Paper 32: Harrison et al. (2019) — Best-Evidence Pediatric Pain Rehabilitation

Citation: Harrison LE, Pate JW, Richardson PA, Ickmans K, Wicksell RK, Simons LE. Best-Evidence for the Rehabilitation of Chronic Pain Part 1: Pediatric Pain. J Clin Med. 2019 Aug 21;8(9):1267.

  • Core Principles of Pediatric Rehabilitation:
    1. Function-First Paradigm: Primary treatment goal is functional restoration (school attendance, sports participation, peer socialization), NOT immediate pain reduction.
    2. Graded In Vivo Exposure (EXPANSE Protocol): Systematically exposes fearful youth to avoided movements and physical activities, disconfirming catastrophized threat expectations.
    3. Parent Coaching & Behavior Modification: Parents are integral co-clients. Treatment targets reducing parental hyper-protectiveness and parental solicitousness (e.g., repeatedly asking about pain, allowing unexcused school absence).
    4. Return to School (RTS) Protocols: Graded, non-negotiable school reintegration schedule developed in direct coordination with school staff, school nurses, and families.

📄 Paper 33: Fisher et al. (2018 & 2019) — Cochrane Reviews on Pediatric Psychological Therapies

  • Fisher et al. (2018) Cochrane Review — Face-to-Face Psychological Therapies:
    • Evaluated 47 RCTs ($N=2,884$).
    • Psychological therapies (predominantly CBT) are highly effective in reducing pain in children with headache ($\text{OR} = 2.47\text{ for }\ge 50\%\text{ pain reduction}$ at follow-up) and abdominal pain.
    • Delivers moderate improvements in anxiety and disability.
  • Fisher et al. (2019) Cochrane Review — Remotely Delivered Psychological Therapies:
    • Evaluated internet and app-delivered psychological treatments.
    • Internet CBT delivers statistically significant reductions in headache and non-headache pain intensity ($\text{RR} = 2.47$), dramatically expanding access to specialized care.

📄 Paper 34: Hatfield & Ely (2015) & Hamill et al. (2014) — Pediatric Assessment & Developmental Stages

  • Developmental Neurobiology of Pediatric Pain:
    • The nociceptive system develops early in gestation (synapses form in dorsal horn by 20–24 weeks).
    • Descending inhibitory pathways (PAG-RVM) mature late (post-natal development). Consequently, premature and newborn infants are more vulnerable to pain and central sensitization than older children or adults.
    • Untreated neonatal pain in the NICU causes structural alterations in pain pathways, heightened long-term stress reactivity, and lowered pain thresholds in adolescence.
  • Age-Appropriate Pain Assessment Tools:
    • Neonates & Pre-verbal Infants (<3 years):
      • NIPS (Neonatal Infant Pain Scale): Facial expression, cry, breathing, arm/leg movements, arousal state.
      • FLACC (Face, Legs, Activity, Cry, Consolability): Validated from 2 months to 7 years.
      • PIPP (Premature Infant Pain Profile): Incorporates gestational age, baseline physiological changes, and facial action coding.
    • Young Children (3 to 7 years):
      • Self-report face scales: Faces Pain Scale - Revised (FPS-R) and Wong-Baker FACES.
    • Children $\ge 8$ years & Adolescents:
      • Standard numeric scales: 0–10 Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) and Visual Analogue Scale (VAS).
    • Pain Location Tools (Hamill et al. 2014):
      • Pediatric body maps / pain drawings are valid and reliable in children aged $\ge 4\text{ to }5\text{ years}$. Children $<4$ years lack spatial body schema mapping.
