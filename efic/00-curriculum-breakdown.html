Section One: Pain Science

1.1 Theories and Mechanisms of Pain

  • 1.1.1 Understand contemporary and evolving theories of pain, their historical context, and their relative usefulness in clinical practice.
  • 1.1.2 Understand the philosophical aspects of pain theory (such as monism versus dualism; reductionism versus holism; and embodied predictive processing).
  • 1.1.3 Demonstrate knowledge and understanding of the transduction, transmission, perception and modulation of nociception and pain including:
  • The characteristics of sensory neurons (e.g., C-fibres and A-fibres).
  • Relevant pathways, structures, receptors, ion-channels, and neurotransmitters.
  • 1.1.4 Discuss the distinction between nociception and pain.
  • 1.1.5 Discuss mechanistic descriptors and classifications of pain (such as 'nociceptive', 'neuropathic' and 'nociplastic' pain) and their relevance to the assessment and management of patients' pain.
  • 1.1.6 Understand the concepts and neurobiological mechanisms underlying peripheral and central sensitisation, primary and secondary hyperalgesia, and the transition from acute to chronic pain.

1.2 The Language of Pain Science

  • 1.2.1 Understand common pain and neuroscience-related phenomena as defined by the IASP terminology (e.g., hyperalgesia, allodynia, sensitisation) and the World Health Organisation (WHO, e.g., ICD-11, ICF).
  • 1.2.2 Understand other contemporary pain-related terminologies (e.g., lived experience of pain, stigmatisation, person-centeredness, and diagnostic uncertainty).

1.3 The Complex, Variable and Multidimensional Nature of Pain

  • 1.3.1 Discuss the pain science underlying the complexity, variability, and multidimensionality of pain:
  • Evolution, application, and limitations of the biopsychosocial model of pain.
  • Aspects of a lived-experience of pain and how the person in pain can be included in the process of assessment and management.
  • Cognitive dimensions of pain (pain-related beliefs, experiences, self-stigmatisation, fear-avoidance, catastrophizing, operant and classical conditioning).
  • Role of lifestyle factors on nociception and pain (physical activity/inactivity, obesity, sleep, substance misuse, stress, trauma/violence).
  • Modifiable and non-modifiable risk factors linked to the development of chronic pain (genetic, socioeconomic, occupational, educational factors, multimorbidity, emotional wellbeing, health literacy, social stigmatisation).
  • Role of contextual factors on pain (placebo, nocebo, therapeutic alliance, healthcare culture).

Section Two: Principles of Assessment and Measurement

2.1 Assessment

  • 2.1.1 Demonstrate the ability to plan and undertake a comprehensive assessment of the person in pain across the lifespan using a biopsychosocial and person-centred approach.
  • 2.1.2 Discuss the rationale behind basic biopsychosocial assessment of a person in pain.
  • 2.1.3 Demonstrate an understanding of how cultural, societal, economic and institutional environments may influence pain assessment.
  • 2.1.4 Demonstrate an understanding of patient and provider factors (e.g., attitudes, beliefs, values, health literacy, affective, cognitive, behavioural responses) that may influence pain assessment.
  • 2.1.5 Demonstrate a person-centred approach that considers biological, functional, psychological, work-related and social aspects of the person in pain.
  • 2.1.6 Demonstrate knowledge of the rationale for and value of basic investigations (such as red flags, serious pathology, neuropathies, musculoskeletal injuries).
  • 2.1.7 Understand the role of specialist assessment, and when it is appropriate to refer for investigation and multidisciplinary / medical management.

2.2 Outcome Measures

  • 2.2.1 Demonstrate an ability to select and interpret valid and reliable pain-specific biopsychosocial outcome measures across the ICF domains to assess, monitor, and modify treatment.
  • 2.2.2 Demonstrate ability to undertake physical assessment (levels of physical activity, function in daily life, functional tests, capacity evaluations, sleep).
  • 2.2.3 Demonstrate an understanding of, and ability to select, valid and reliable tools to monitor the effect of pain management interventions across the lifespan and in special populations (pediatric, older adults, cognitive impairment, cultural/linguistic diversity).

Section Three: Principles of Treatment

3.1 Foundations of Treatment

  • 3.1.1 Demonstrate the professional responsibility of treating all patients with dignity and respect, irrespective of background and status.
  • 3.1.2 Develop, justify, and negotiate with the patient an individually tailored treatment plan based on evidence, clinical reasoning, and patient preferences.
  • 3.1.3 Understand and apply the principles of evidence-based practice and research when planning and implementing treatment.
  • 3.1.4 Discuss the importance of proactively, systematically, and continually evaluating pain management outcomes.
  • 3.1.5 Understand and differentiate between multimodal and multidisciplinary pain management.

3.2 Treatment Effects

  • 3.2.1 Understand the range of treatment effects and outcomes that can occur (positive, negative, adverse, unpredicted, nocebo/placebo, specific vs non-specific).
  • 3.2.2 Understand the balance of treatment effects and side-effects.

3.3 Communication

  • 3.3.1 Demonstrate the ability to communicate empathetically, respectfully, and effectively with people in pain and significant others.
  • 3.3.2 Apply communication strategies to facilitate shared decision-making, patient self-efficacy, and therapeutic alliance.
  • 3.3.3 Formulate and discuss a prognosis and realistic goals with patients.

3.4 Education

  • 3.4.1 Demonstrate ability to use contemporary science-based explanations (e.g., neurophysiology / PNE) with patients and carers.
  • 3.4.2 Provide and discuss appropriate information about a range of pain management interventions.
  • 3.4.3 Awareness of service delivery modes (online, group education, 1-on-1).
  • 3.4.4 Apply educational strategies that enhance health literacy, active engagement, and supported self-management.

3.5 Behavioural Therapies

  • 3.5.1 Demonstrate knowledge of behavioural therapies (CBT, ACT, MBSR, relaxation, autogenic training, graded exposure, graded activity).
  • 3.5.2 Identify psychological risk factors (Yellow Flags) and adapt physiotherapy treatment accordingly.

3.6 Physical Activity and Exercise Prescription

  • 3.6.1 Demonstrate and apply evidence-based physical activity and exercise prescription in chronic pain.
  • 3.6.2 Understand barriers and health benefits of physical activity for people in pain.
  • 3.6.3 Modify physical activity based on pain state, age, comorbidities, pregnancy, dementia, mental health issues.

3.7 Digital Health Management

  • 3.7.1 Discuss opportunities, limitations, and ethical issues associated with digital health technologies (apps, wearables, telehealth, virtual reality).

3.8 Work

  • 3.8.1 Understand the importance of work for physical, psychological, and financial health.
  • 3.8.2 Discuss factors influencing work capability, absence, and return-to-work (Blue and Black flags).
  • 3.8.3 Apply return-to-work strategies in collaboration with patients and employers.

3.9 Referral & Multidisciplinary Collaboration

  • 3.9.1 Collaborate across multidisciplinary teams in health and social care.
  • 3.9.2 Understand indications, efficacy, complications, and evidence for procedural modalities (injections, surgery, neuromodulation/SCS).
  • 3.9.3 Demonstrate knowledge of analgesic pharmacological classes (indications, mechanisms, side-effects, interactions with exercise, opioid-induced hyperalgesia).

Section Four: Populations in Pain

4.1 Epidemiology

  • 4.1.1 Demonstrate knowledge of epidemiology of chronic pain (incidence, prevalence, societal impact).
  • 4.1.2 Understand socio-demographic factors (age, gender, ethnicity, socioeconomic status) on health inequalities.
  • 4.1.3 Understand risk, prognostic, and predictive factors for the transition from acute to chronic pain.

4.2 Pain Assessment and Management for Specific Populations

  • 4.2.1 Adapt assessment and management across life stages (infants, children, adolescents, working age, older adults).
  • 4.2.2 Specific clinical conditions:
  • Musculoskeletal pain (spinal, peripheral joint, tendinopathy, myofascial).
  • Osteoarthritis.
  • Inflammatory and autoimmune arthropathies.
  • Chronic widespread pain / Fibromyalgia.
  • Chronic post-surgical and post-traumatic pain.
  • Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS).
  • Neuropathic pain (peripheral and central).
  • Pelvic pain conditions.
  • Headache and orofacial pain.
  • Cancer-related pain.
  • Visceral pain.
  • 4.2.3 Vulnerable groups (cognitive impairment / dementia, non-communicative patients, trauma/torture survivors, addiction/substance use).

Section Five: Interprofessional Working and Learning

5.1 Interprofessional Working and Learning

  • 5.1.1 Discuss the importance of interprofessional working along with barriers and facilitators.
  • 5.1.2 Work in partnership with patients, carers, multidisciplinary team members and agencies.
  • 5.1.3 Engage in regular interprofessional education, peer supervision, and reflective practice.
